# Model Excel Energy, prove yourself without projects

The single best site for two electrician brothers to model a UK commercial solar and PPA website on is **Excel Energy (excelenergy.co.uk)**: a post-2020 commercial installer on WordPress and Elementor that built its credibility from a 15-item accreditations page with one-line explanations, a named PPA funder (Smart Ease) quoted with real p/kWh figures, eight sector pages, an ungated calculator and a "Book a FREE Desktop Consultation" call to action, while featuring only three case studies ([Excel Energy](https://excelenergy.co.uk/); [Excel accreditations](https://excelenergy.co.uk/renewable-energy-solutions/accreditations/)). The runner-up is **Ecoaim**, a "110+ installs" Scottish firm whose process page promises a fixed-price quote within 24 hours and MCS handover within five working days ([Ecoaim process](https://ecoaim.co.uk/process/)). The stronger answer, though, is a hybrid: Excel's page set and trust wall, Ecoaim's process page and tone, Photon Energy's commercial-only positioning and named-funder PPA page, Spirit Energy and Eden Sustainable's finance content, and the PPA-page pattern of the Australian electrical contractor Select Electrical, none of which needs a portfolio. What may be imitated is the structure, page set, section order, navigation patterns and tone, because "a competitor can build a site in a similar style or with a similar structure provided they do not copy your original content"; what must not be copied is text, photographs, icons, code, logos, testimonials, client logos and savings claims, and a UK home-improvement firm paid **£6,300** for lifting 21 photographs ([Sprintlaw](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/website-copyright-in-the-uk-what-it-covers-and-how-to-protect-it/); [Swan Turton on Absolute Lofts v Artisan](https://swanturton.com/additional-damages-of-20x-the-licence-fee-for-infringement-of-loft-conversion-photographs-absolute-lofts-v-artisan-home-improvements/)). The no-portfolio problem is solved by honesty plus substitutes: describe the subcontract work as the founders' experience with the role stated and an evidence file behind every number (CAP Code rule 3.7), seek written permission from the main contractor, and lean on NICEIC/NAPIT registration, insurance certificates, MCS (2–4 months, about £1,044 first year via NICEIC), an SSIP badge, free manufacturer certifications, a timed process page and modelled example systems labelled as such ([ASA/CAP substantiation](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation.html); [NICEIC MCS scheme](https://niceic.com/for-the-trades-1/professional-standards/schemes/mcs-scheme/)). A 90-day plan turns the first sub-50 kWp install into both the MCS assessment job and case study one. The practical build is Webflow Premium at $25/month with the $129 Energy X template and two to four weeks of freelancer time, or WordPress with the $69 iRecco theme through a UK renewables freelancer at £800–£3,000; first-year all-in costs run from roughly £500–£900 for pure DIY to £4,000–£8,000 for a local agency ([Memberstack on Webflow pricing](https://www.memberstack.com/blog/new-webflow-pricing-in-2026-what-every-plan-costs-and-how-to-choose); [Podium Design](https://www.podiumdesign.co.uk/services/renewable-energy-website-design)). The PPA page must be built around the partner-funded model: a 25-year norm, year-one rates of roughly 9–18p/kWh with a 2–3% or index-linked escalator, a 100 kWp and 80,000–100,000 kWh entry threshold, and never the word "free" ([solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk providers](https://solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk/providers/); [Spirit Energy PPA](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/ppa-solar); [ASA/CAP on "free"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/use-of-free.html)).

## One primary model, one runner-up, and why a hybrid beats both

**Model the site on Excel Energy, with Ecoaim as runner-up.** Excel Energy is the right primary for three reasons that map directly onto the brothers' situation. First, it is a young company (company number 14127261, a 2022-series registration) that reads as national without a big portfolio: its homepage stats are modest ("30 years of combined industry experience", a "5-year workmanship warranty", 22 Google reviews) and only three named projects are featured, yet the site carries a complete commercial page set and the most complete accreditation wall in the UK sample ([Excel Energy](https://excelenergy.co.uk/); [Excel accreditations](https://excelenergy.co.uk/renewable-energy-solutions/accreditations/)). Second, it already solves the PPA-without-capital problem the way the brothers must: it is an "approved Smart Ease Channel Partner" and quotes the funder's real terms, a PPA at "typically £0.09−£0.15/kWh" over "7 to 30 years", suited to businesses with "monthly power bills of £1,500 or more", with instant approval "up to £150K" ([Excel financing](https://excelenergy.co.uk/commercial-solar-financing/)). Third, it runs on WordPress and Elementor, the most common and cheapest build path with the broadest UK freelancer pool, so the structure can be reproduced without an agency ([Excel Energy](https://excelenergy.co.uk/)). Its two weaknesses are easy to avoid: the all-caps SEO headline ("EXCEL ENERGY - YOUR UK COMMERCIAL SOLAR PANEL & RENEWABLE ENERGY INSTALLERS") and the absence of a finance comparison table.

Ecoaim earns the runner-up spot because it is the closest in scale to a two-person firm and the best UK example of credibility without a large portfolio. Its process page ("From first call to MCS certificate, here's exactly what you should expect") promises a "Fixed-price quote within 24 hours" and "MCS certification & handover" "within 5 working days of install"; its sectors hub lists eight sectors, each with six to nine town-level pages; and its 2026 PPA article costs a 250 kWp system three ways (CapEx £200,000 upfront with a ~3-year payback; lease at £0 upfront; PPA at £0 upfront with a ~£29,440 year-one saving) and states honestly that "for a profit-making, cash-rich business, CapEx almost always wins over 25 years" while a PPA "is frequently the smarter call" for organisations conserving capital ([Ecoaim process](https://ecoaim.co.uk/process/); [Ecoaim sectors](https://ecoaim.co.uk/sectors/); [Ecoaim PPA article](https://ecoaim.co.uk/blog/funding-solar-with-ppa-uk/)). Its drawback as a whole-site model is that its commercial PPA explanation lives in a blog post rather than a product page, its title tag leads with domestic pricing, and it is an Astro static build that a non-developer cannot edit ([Ecoaim](https://ecoaim.co.uk/)).

The hybrid is the better answer because no single site contains every block the brothers need. The recommended assembly is: Excel Energy's navigation, accreditations page and partner-funded finance pages as the skeleton; Ecoaim's timed process page, Commercial/Domestic split and plain-English "when a PPA is right and when it isn't" tone; Photon Energy's commercial-only statement and its practice of naming the funder ("TBS funds 100% of the procurement, installation and ongoing maintenance") on the PPA page ([Photon PPA](https://www.photonenergy.co.uk/power-purchase-agreement)); Spirit Energy's finance-page section order (three routes, how each works, returns, leased premises, tax reliefs, FAQs, downloadable guide) and its numeric PPA eligibility block ([Spirit finance](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/finance); [Spirit PPA](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/ppa-solar)); Eden Sustainable's PPA page order (contract, cost, installation, end of term, balance sheet, evidence, PPA vs self-funded, FAQs) as the content template for explaining a partner-funded PPA ([Eden PPA](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa)); Select Electrical's PPA page shape (definition, term, saving, ownership transfer, four-step graphic, five FAQs, licence numbers) as the small-electrical-contractor precedent ([Select PPA](https://selectelectricalonline.com.au/commercial-solar-ppa/)); SunPeak's navigation built from writable pages and Fort Energy's accreditation-and-outcomes trust block for the "no proof yet" phase ([SunPeak](https://www.sunpeakpower.com/); [Fort Energy](https://www.fortenergy.ie/)); and Todae's system-size package page, rewritten for UK 50, 100 and 250 kWp systems, as the most transferable "ROI example" device for a firm with no case studies, because its numbers are generic to a system size rather than to a client ([Todae 100kW page](https://www.todaesolar.com.au/commercial-solar-systems/100kw-solar/)). Geo Green Power's footer supplies the last piece, an FCA Introducer Appointed Representative line, once the firm has a credit-broker relationship for asset finance ([Geo Green finance](https://www.geogreenpower.com/finance-options-for-solar-projects-commercial-funding-and-solar-business-grants/)).

### What may be imitated and what must not be copied

"Copying" a site lawfully in the UK means wireframing its page architecture and section order as a list of ideas, writing every sentence from scratch, sourcing your own imagery, building from a purchased template so no CSS, JavaScript or icon files are lifted, and choosing your own palette, typography and logo so the overall get-up is not confusable. The legal basis is that UK copyright automatically protects the expression on a site, meaning "Written content and blog posts", "Photographs, illustrations, logos and graphics", "Web page layouts and original design elements", videos and "Software code and scripts", but "Copyright protects the expression of ideas, not the ideas themselves", so "a competitor can build a site in a similar style or with a similar structure provided they do not copy your original content" ([Sprintlaw, website copyright](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/website-copyright-in-the-uk-what-it-covers-and-how-to-protect-it/)). In *Navitaire v easyJet* the court refused broad "look and feel" protection and held that "business logic" and functional effects are not protected, while specific icons and GUI screen layouts were artistic works that were infringed when copied; *THJ v Sheridan* (Court of Appeal, 2023) confirmed that even low-creativity screen designs attract copyright, but with correspondingly narrow scope so that only close copies infringe ([5RB, Navitaire](https://www.5rb.com/navitaire-inc-v-easyjet-airline-co-another/); [Kluwer, THJ v Sheridan](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/uk-court-of-appeal-rules-on-copyright-in-guis/)). A colour scheme on its own is low-risk, but the same colours plus the same fonts, layout, imagery style and naming could support a passing-off claim, which protects "the whole external appearance or look-and-feel" where it misrepresents a connection between businesses ([Wikipedia, passing off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passing_off)). Copied text is also the fastest way to get caught, and Google's spam policy demotes sites that republish "content from other sites without adding any original content or value" ([Google spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)).

| Element of the reference site | Imitate? | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Page structure and section order (hero, sectors, process, finance, FAQ, CTA) | Yes, as ideas re-implemented from scratch | Ideas and business logic unprotected ([Sprintlaw](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/website-copyright-in-the-uk-what-it-covers-and-how-to-protect-it/); [Navitaire](https://www.5rb.com/navitaire-inc-v-easyjet-airline-co-another/)) |
| Navigation patterns, mega-menu, sticky CTA, accordion FAQ, calculator concept | Yes | Functional effects fall outside copyright; look-and-feel is "difficult ground" ([Copyright Aid](https://copyrightaid.co.uk/forum/viewtopic.php?t=2109)) |
| Page set, tone of voice, CTA wording style ("Book a free desktop consultation") | Yes, in your own words | Names and short phrases are not protected by copyright ([Sprintlaw, infringement](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/copyright-infringement-in-the-uk-what-businesses-should-know/)) |
| Colour scheme alone | Generally yes; avoid the same colour + font + layout + imagery combination | Passing off protects overall get-up ([Wikipedia, passing off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passing_off)) |
| Page copy, headlines, service descriptions, FAQs | No, rewrite entirely | A copied paragraph "is likely" to infringe ([Fosters Solicitors](https://fosters-solicitors.co.uk/insight/plagiarism-and-unfair-association-by-competitors)) |
| Photographs, videos, drone footage | No | £300 licence fee plus £6,000 additional damages for 21 copied photos ([8 New Square](https://8newsquare.co.uk/absolute-lofts-south-west-london-ltd-v-artisan-home-improvements-ltd-2015-ewhc-2608-ipec/)) |
| Illustrations, icons, infographics, bespoke UI graphics | No | GUI icons and screen layouts are artistic works ([Navitaire, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navitaire_Inc_v_Easyjet_Airline_Co._and_BulletProof_Technologies,_Inc.)) |
| HTML/CSS/JS, theme files | No wholesale copying; buying the same template is fine | Code is a literary work ([GOV.UK copyright](https://www.gov.uk/copyright)) |
| Logo, company name, taglines, look-alike domains | No | Trade marks and passing off ([Sprintlaw, copied website](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/someone-copied-my-entire-website/)) |
| Case-study data, testimonials, reviews, client logos | No | Fabricated endorsements prohibited under the DMCC Act 2024; misleading B2B claims under the BPRs 2008 ([HWB Accountants](https://www.hwb-accountants.com/news-guides/news/legal-requirements-for-company-websites-in-the-uk-2026-guide/); [Sprintlaw, BPMMR](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/misleading-marketing-regulations-2008-business-protection/)) |
| Savings and performance claims, calculator numbers | No, build your own evidence base | Claims must be substantiated and imagery must match ([ASA, savings claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/bill-ieve-it-or-not-making-greener-homes-savings-claims-add-up.html)) |
| Manufacturer product images seen on the reference site | No, obtain from the manufacturer under its own terms | e.g. [SMA logo licence](https://policy.sma.energy/en/logo-usage); [K2 press images](https://k2-systems.com/en/company/press-media/) |

Two further cautions follow from the notes. Copying a competitor's savings or performance claims imports a substantiation problem the new firm cannot meet: the ASA upheld a complaint against Centrica Hive's "Shrink your electricity bills by up to 94%" because only 44% of customers achieved it and the modelled basis was material information that had to be in the ad ([ASA, Centrica Hive ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/centrica-hive-ltd-a25-1295069-centrica-hive-ltd.html)). And because the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court awards additional damages for flagrancy and has a small-claims track, a copied-photo dispute realistically costs a small firm several thousand pounds plus legal costs rather than a licence fee; image-licensing agencies (Pixsy, Copytrack, PicRights) also send demand letters to UK small businesses in the hundreds to low thousands of pounds ([Trust Your Website](https://trustyourwebsite.com/uk/en/guides/copytrack-picright-legit)). The researchers found no UK judgment squarely on website "look and feel" and no dispute over a copied solar site, so the position above is general information and the founders should have a solicitor confirm anything contentious.

## A fourteen-page outline that converts before the first project exists

The best UK commercial sites converge on a seven-part top level, Solutions/Technologies, Sectors, Finance/Funding, Case Studies, About (with Accreditations and Team), Resources (FAQs, guides, calculators) and Contact/Get a Quote, with commercial and domestic split at the top of the menu when both are served; sub-pages almost always include Solar PV, Battery Storage, EV Charging and O&M under Solutions, and CAPEX, PPA and asset finance under Finance ([EvoEnergy](https://evoenergy.co.uk/); [Spirit Energy](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/); [Excel Energy](https://excelenergy.co.uk/); [Harvest Green](https://www.harvestgreendevelopments.co.uk/)). The international pattern is the same conversion path in a different accent: benefit hero, "book a free assessment", three finance cards, sectors, process, proof, FAQ, assessment form again, and a new firm "can build every block except 'proof' on day one and substitute licences, accreditations, named funder and a documented process for the portfolio" ([Todae feasibility check](https://www.todaesolar.com.au/commercial-solar-feasibility-check/); [Smart Commercial Energy process](https://www.smartcommercialenergy.com.au/our-process)). Four conversion rules from the design evidence shape every page below. The primary call to action should be "Book a free site survey" or "Request a feasibility check" rather than "Get a free quote", because a UK SEO consultancy reports that "'Free quote buttons' now read as sales traps, not offers" and the CTA should describe what actually happens next ([Lillian Purge](https://www.lillianpurge.co.uk/the-complete-seo-guide-for-solar-companies-in-the-uk); [SurgePV, 20 changes](https://www.surgepv.com/blog/solar-website-conversion)). First-touch forms should be short: one solar case reports that cutting a form from seven fields to three "increased form completion by 145%" and halved cost per lead, with optimal fields of name, phone, postcode and optional monthly electricity spend, while qualification dropdowns cost some conversion but improve lead quality ([Web Tonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/solar-energy-landing-page-statistics); [involve.me](https://www.involve.me/blog/landing-page-statistics)). Speed to lead is the cheapest lever a two-person firm has, since "78% of solar customers choose the first company that responds to their inquiry" and "55% of companies take five or more days to respond" ([Callpage](https://www.callpage.io/blog/posts/strategies-for-solar-lead-generation)). And each high-intent page should ask for one decision, with certification badges placed next to the CTA rather than in the footer ([SurgePV, 20 changes](https://www.surgepv.com/blog/solar-website-conversion); [Diamond Group](https://www.diamond-group.co/blog/how-solar-companies-build-trust-before-the-first-call)).

Global elements apply to every page: a header with the logo, a seven-item nav (Commercial Solar | Solar PPA | Sectors | How It Works | About | Resources | Contact), a clickable phone number and a sticky "Book a free site survey" button; and a footer with name, address and phone, opening hours, areas served (linked to area pages), accreditation logos, review-platform links, the statutory company details and LocalBusiness schema ([Sunvoy](https://sunvoy.com/blog/solar-marketing-website); [Lillian Purge](https://www.lillianpurge.co.uk/the-complete-seo-guide-for-solar-companies-in-the-uk)). Ad landing pages can drop the top navigation, which one compilation reports lifts landing-page conversion by 10–25%, but the main site should keep it ([Web Tonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/solar-energy-landing-page-statistics)).

### The PPA page: partner-funded, numeric, and never "free"

A two-person installer cannot fund PPAs itself, so the page must describe the model the market actually runs on: the installer originates and designs the project and is paid as EPC contractor, the funder pays for, owns, insures and maintains the array and holds the PPA, and the customer buys the solar kWh at a fixed price. Spirit Energy describes exactly this split ("The PPA investor funds the system design and installation, retaining ownership throughout the agreement term. Spirit provides the system design, installation, monitoring and ongoing maintenance services on behalf of the investor"), and UrbanVolt's EPC programme states that the funder provides "Funding, PPA contract, asset monitoring" while the EPC provides "System design, installation, client relationship management" and "You retain the customer relationship throughout" ([Spirit PPA](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/ppa-solar); [UrbanVolt EPC guide](https://urbanvolt.com/guides/how-solar-epcs-unlock-funded-pipeline-with-a-ppa/)). The verified market parameters to build the page around are a **25-year norm** (Eden, Spirit and AMPYR all state 25 years, with 10–25 years the most common published range), year-one rates of roughly **9–18p/kWh** by size (Smart Ease "approx. £0.09−£0.15/kWh"; an SEO aggregator's 2026 table runs from 16–20p at 50 kWp down to 10–13p at 1 MWp against a 21–25p grid benchmark), an escalator that is "typically between 2% and 3% per year" or RPI/CPI-linked, funder-borne monitoring, maintenance, inverter replacement and insurance, and three end-of-term options: buy at a nominal value, extend, or have the system removed and the roof reinstated ([Eden PPA](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa); [Smart Ease FAQ](https://smartease.uk/faqs-business-customers/); [solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk cost page](https://solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk/cost/); [Ecoaim PPA article](https://ecoaim.co.uk/blog/funding-solar-with-ppa-uk/)). Published discount-versus-grid claims range from 10% to 50%, so the honest framing is that the rate is set at a discount to the customer's current grid price and the size of the discount depends on the site; year-one comparisons flatter the deal if the escalator is ignored, since the aggregator's worked example shows a fixed 2% escalator adding £245,000 and CPI at 2.5% adding £316,000 over a 20-year, 500 kWp contract starting at 12p ([solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk cost page](https://solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk/cost/)).

The compliance constraints are specific. The CAP Code applies to business-to-business marketing alongside the Business Protection from Misleading Marketing Regulations 2008, so "it's B2B" is not a defence ([ASA/CAP Appendix 1](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/appendix-1.html)). Rule 3.23 means nothing may be described as "free" if the customer pays anything beyond unavoidable costs, and under a PPA the customer pays per kWh for 25 years, so "free solar panels" is out and "no upfront cost, funded by our partner" is in ([ASA/CAP on "free"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/use-of-free.html)). "Save up to X%" needs evidence that a significant proportion of comparable sites achieve X, and modelled assumptions are material information that must appear in the ad itself ([ASA/CAP savings claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/bill-ieve-it-or-not-making-greener-homes-savings-claims-add-up.html)). A behind-the-meter PPA is an electricity supply contract, not a credit agreement, so the PPA itself needs no FCA wording; the funder, not the installer, is the exempt supplier under the automatic Class A exemption, so the site should never describe the firm as "supplying" electricity ([DESNZ licence exemptions FAQ](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/658d83aa01760d00135cf8d8/electricity-licence-exemptions-guidance.pdf)). Any mention of hire purchase, leasing or loans does need the introducer wording and a named regulated broker, as Geo Green Power's footer shows ("Introducer Appointed Representative ... of Phoenix Financial Consultants Limited") ([Geo Green finance](https://www.geogreenpower.com/finance-options-for-solar-projects-commercial-funding-and-solar-business-grants/)). The tax comparison must also be right: companies buying outright get 100% relief on up to £1m via the Annual Investment Allowance, but solar panels are special-rate assets, so above that the 50% first-year allowance applies rather than full expensing, and a PPA customer gets no capital allowances at all because the funder owns the asset ([HMRC HS252](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/capital-allowances-and-balancing-charges-hs252-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs252-capital-allowances-and-balancing-charges-2024); [Excel Energy finance article](https://excelenergy.co.uk/news/how-to-finance-commercial-solar-panels-in-the-uk/)).

The page should run in twelve blocks, drawn from the highest-quality UK pages (Eden's key takeaways, cost, end of term, balance sheet and PPA-vs-self-funded sections; Spirit's numeric eligibility; Commercial Solar UK's "Who Qualifies" and "Risks and Guarantees"; Solar4Business's named funder and comparison table) ([Eden PPA](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa); [Spirit PPA](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/ppa-solar); [Commercial Solar UK PPA](https://commercialsolar.uk.com/services/solar-ppa/); [Solar4Business](https://solar4business.uk/commercial-solar-ppa/)). First, a hero: H1 "Commercial solar with no upfront cost, funded by our PPA partners", sub-line "We design and install the system. A specialist funder pays for it, owns it and maintains it. You buy the power it generates at a fixed price per kWh set below your current grid rate, typically for 25 years", with CTAs "Check if your site qualifies" and a phone number. Second, a three-item proof strip: "£0 upfront, £0 maintenance"; "Fixed p/kWh rate agreed before install (rises by a small fixed % or index each year)"; "Options to own, extend or remove at the end". Third, the numeric eligibility checklist below. Fourth, a six-step "how it works": free desktop assessment using half-hourly data and the roof; design and PPA offers from funding partners (typically more than one compared, as Spirit obtains "three or four PPA quotes"); a fixed p/kWh offer with escalator and term stated; contracts (PPA plus roof lease or licence with the funder, landlord consent if leased); install and commissioning with the G99 application handled; the funder monitors, maintains and insures while the customer pays monthly for solar kWh used, with "typically 14–20 weeks from assessment to commissioning" as Eden states ([Spirit PPA](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/ppa-solar); [Eden PPA](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa)). Fifth, "what you pay and what's included": only solar electricity used on site at the agreed rate; grid top-up continues from the existing supplier; the escalator stated; monitoring, maintenance, inverter replacement and insurance covered by the funder; surplus export normally belonging to the funder; a worked example labelled "illustrative, based on stated assumptions" rather than a bare "save up to 40%". Sixth, end-of-term options with the line "depends on the funder; we show you the exact clause before you sign". Seventh, the comparison table (Buy outright / Asset finance or lease / Solar PPA against upfront cost, ownership, who maintains and insures, tax relief, typical term, balance sheet, lifetime saving, best for) and a decision rule: cash-rich and staying put, buy; want ownership but preserve cash, finance; no capex and a fixed unit rate, PPA. Eighth, an honesty block: a 15–25-year commitment; a roof lease or licence granted to the funder; a sale or relocation means the PPA transfers to the new occupier or is terminated under the contract, and early termination charges may reference "the provider's lost revenue over the remaining term, which may make termination expensive"; the funder, not the installer, is the counterparty for the solar kWh; savings depend on self-consumption ([Sprintlaw, PPAs](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/power-purchase-agreements-for-solar-installations-in-the-uk/); [Eden PPA](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa)). Ninth, "who we work with", naming only funders the firm has actually signed with. Tenth, a case study or, until one exists, an "example 150 kWp warehouse" clearly labelled as modelled. Eleventh, eight to ten FAQs (Is it really no upfront cost? Who owns the panels? What if I sell the building? What if the system underperforms? Can I add batteries later? Is my business too small? Can tenants do this? What happens to export? Does the price change? Can I buy the system early?). Twelfth, the suitability form (annual kWh, tenure, roof area or building size, years trading, postcode, optional bill upload) with the CTA "Check if my site qualifies" and a secondary link "Prefer to buy outright? See our commercial solar page".

The plain-English explanation the researchers drafted for block one and five can be used nearly verbatim: "A Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) lets your business run on solar without buying the system. A specialist funder pays for the panels, inverters and installation, owns the equipment and looks after it for the length of the agreement, usually 25 years. We survey your site, design the system and install it. Under the PPA you buy the electricity the panels generate and you use on site, at a fixed price per kWh agreed before installation and set below what you currently pay the grid. The rate normally rises by a small fixed percentage (typically 2–3%) or in line with an inflation index each year, and the agreement says exactly how. Your existing electricity supplier keeps supplying whatever the panels do not cover. Monitoring, maintenance, repairs, inverter replacement and insurance are the funder's responsibility, so there is nothing to budget for. Because the funder owns the panels, they usually take the income from any surplus exported to the grid, and they will need a lease or licence over the roof area for the term. At the end of the agreement you can usually buy the system for a pre-agreed (often nominal) sum, extend the agreement, or ask for the equipment to be removed and the roof reinstated. If you move out or sell the building, the agreement can normally pass to the new occupier if they agree to it; otherwise the contract's early-termination terms apply, so read them before you sign. There is no upfront cost, but it is a long-term contract with a third party, and your savings depend on how much of the solar power you use on site." Every clause in that paragraph traces to a published funder or installer page cited above.

The eligibility checklist should be headed "Typical funder requirements; we confirm eligibility with our funding partners", because thresholds vary by funder and Carbon3 notes that a PPA "is not government funded" so there is suitability rather than strict eligibility ([Carbon3](https://carbon3.co.uk/carbon3-guide-to-ppa-what-is-a-power-purchase-agreement-uk/)). The six lines, each anchored to a published criterion, are: roof or ground space for a system of about **100 kWp or more** (roughly 600 m² of unshaded roof), with some funders considering sites from about 70 kW (Spirit "100 kWp or more (around 600 m² of roof space as a guide)"; Smart Ease "minimum 70kW"; UrbanVolt "100kWp up to 3MW"); annual electricity use of about **80,000–100,000 kWh or more** with most consumption in daylight hours and a half-hourly meter (Spirit "at least 80,000 kWh"; Flame Energy "approximately 100,000 kWh or more"); freehold ownership, or a lease with **15 or more years** left plus the landlord's consent (Commercial Solar UK "minimum 15-year term"; Eden's leased-buildings guidance on a "License to Alter"); **two or more years' filed accounts** and a sound credit profile, since funders credit-check the business that will buy the power (Smart Ease "CRN for 2+ years"; UrbanVolt "score 75 or above through Experian"); a structurally sound roof with 25 years of life left and a grid connection that can accept the system, with the G99 application handled by the installer; and willingness to enter a 15–25-year agreement and grant the funder a roof lease or licence ([Spirit PPA](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/ppa-solar); [Smart Ease FAQ](https://smartease.uk/faqs-business-customers/); [UrbanVolt partners](https://urbanvolt.com/partners/); [Flame Energy](https://flameenergy.co.uk/solar-panels/fully-funded-solar/); [Commercial Solar UK PPA](https://commercialsolar.uk.com/services/solar-ppa/); [Eden, leased buildings](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/news/can-commercial-solar-panels-be-installed-on-leased-buildings)). A "suitability checker" built from these inputs is more honest than a savings calculator, because PPA pricing is funder-set; the only live PPA rate calculator found in the UK sample belongs to the funder platform MTFD, which quoted "13.47p/kWh for 25yr term at £650/kWp EPC" as an example ([MTFD](https://mtfd.co.uk/)).

The funders to approach, in order, are the ones that publish an installer route: Smart Ease, whose payment plans are available only via "Accredited Channel Partners", which pays installers "within 48 hours of installation" and which since August 2025 wraps Zestec (Octopus Energy Generation) PPAs in an installer package covering "instant finance proposals ... funding, installation and long-term system monitoring and maintenance"; UrbanVolt, with a published EPC partner programme for 100 kWp–3 MW and a lighter "sales partner" variant; Electron Green, "investing up to £1 billion into free solar systems for the C&I sector" and delivered with installers such as 3S Northampton; AMPYR Distributed Energy and its group companies Eden Sustainable and Shawton, which run "a carefully selected ecosystem of developers, EPCs and operational partners" and funded a 224 kW array delivered by a specialist installer; Two Blues Solar, which works with Photon Energy; and Finlight (the ex-Atrato business Brookfield acquired for £220 million with a plan to invest around £1 billion in UK rooftop solar by 2030), which says it collaborates "with EPC contractors" ([Smart Ease channel partners](https://smartease.uk/energy-2/); [Zestec–Smart Ease partnership](https://zestecgroup.co.uk/zestec-partner-with-smart-ease-to-unlock-easier-access-to-funded-commercial-solar/); [UrbanVolt partners](https://urbanvolt.com/partners/); [Electron Green](https://electrongreen.com/); [3S Northampton](https://3sltd.co.uk/ppa/); [AMPYR DE](https://www.ampyrde.com/what-we-do/); [Solar Power Portal, AMPYR 224 kW](https://www.solarpowerportal.co.uk/solar-projects/ampyr-distributed-energy-completes-224kw-rooftop-array); [Photon PPA](https://www.photonenergy.co.uk/power-purchase-agreement); [Finlight](https://finlight.com/); [Solar Power Portal, Brookfield–Atrato](https://www.solarpowerportal.co.uk/solar-investment/brookfield-acquires-atrato-onsite-energy-in-220-million-deal)). Utility-scale developers and supplier PPA desks (Innova, ILOS, Enviromena, Bluefield, Drax, BayWa r.e.) do not fund sub-1 MW rooftops and belong off the list, and Centrica Business Solutions self-delivers at 1 MW+ so it is a competitor rather than a partner ([Centrica Business Solutions](https://www.centricabusinesssolutions.com/blogpost/kick-start-your-net-zero-pathway-solar-ppa)). Funders will still want evidence of competence before the first funded job: UrbanVolt requires "Tier 1 panels and inverters with long warranties", HSE compliance with "stop-work authority", "Clear pricing, staged payments, and full traceability on all major equipment" and two years of preventative maintenance, and a PPA provider directory says EPC requirements "include MCS and NICEIC accreditation" ([UrbanVolt EPC guide](https://urbanvolt.com/guides/how-solar-epcs-unlock-funded-pipeline-with-a-ppa/); [solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk providers](https://solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk/providers/)). ## Credibility without a portfolio: state the role, keep the evidence, get permission

The founders can say more about their subcontract experience than they might expect, provided the overall impression is truthful. Under the CAP Code, which applies to company websites and to business-to-business marketing, marketers must "hold documentary evidence to prove claims that consumers are likely to regard as objective", must not "materially mislead", must not "omit material information" and must not exaggerate capability; the ASA judges "the likely interpretation of a claim, rather than the marketer's intention" ([ASA/CAP substantiation](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation.html); [ASA/CAP misleading advertising](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/misleading-advertising.html)). Applied to this case, "our founders have installed X MWp across Y commercial sites as subcontractors to a national installer" is defensible if the brothers hold records (timesheets, invoices, commissioning sheets, G99 paperwork, dated photos, panel counts) and the sentence makes the subcontract role clear; vagueness about *who* the clients were is fine, but vagueness about *the company's role* is what misleads by omission. The trading-history trap is the other edge: the ASA ruled against Coys' "Founded 1919" claim because the words were "likely to be read as providing assurance ... that Coys had a long trading history, denoting financial stability and inherited goodwill and expertise" when the trading entity dated from 2003, and CAP notes consumers "might be persuaded by claims that a company is well-established ... especially in industries that might have a high level of failed or bankrupted companies" ([Osborne Clarke on Coys](https://marketinglaw.osborneclarke.com/advertising-regulation/founded-1919-classic-car-business-challenges-asa/)). So the new solar company must not borrow Murray Electrical's founding year unless it trades as the same legal entity. MCS adds a third constraint: the Installer Agreement forbids "knowingly making false or misleading statements regarding Your certification status", and a UK commercial-buyer guide lists "No MCS certification (or 'we're applying')" as a kill-the-tender red flag, so until certified the site should say nothing about MCS at all ([MCS Installer Agreement](https://mcscertified.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/MCS-Installer-Agreement-1.0-1.9.2025.pdf); [Solar Panels for Businesses, how to choose](https://solarpanelsforbusinesses.co.uk/blog/how-to-choose-commercial-solar-installer-uk-business/)).

The cleanest formula for a new company with an experienced team comes from real UK examples. JTD Solar (Bristol) is described as "Though the limited company was incorporated in 2024, the core team brings 10 + years of design-and-build experience"; Generate Solar EV (Nottingham) leads with "We're not a sales company. We're a team of installers, electricians, and engineers with decades of hands-on experience"; Actismart (Bilston), founded in 2023 by three electrician brothers, built credibility from Checkatrade, City & Guilds, MCS, NICEIC and IWA logos, three named video testimonials and a "Start-Up Business of the Year Award"; and the US firm Experienced Solar names its founder's 16-year predecessor company outright ([Perfect Sense, Bristol installers](https://perfectsenseenergy.com/solar-insight/top-solar-installers-in-bristol/); [Generate Solar EV](https://generate-solarev.co.uk/); [Actismart](https://actismart.co.uk/why-us/); [Experienced Solar](https://www.experiencedsolar.net/about)). None of the UK examples quantifies pre-founding MWp, which is the brothers' opportunity to differentiate. **Written permission from the main contractor is strongly advisable**, even though truthful, non-confidential statements do not strictly require it, because it removes three risks at once: a confidentiality or publicity clause in the subcontract, an implied-endorsement or passing-off claim, and damage to a relationship the brothers may still need. The risk is real in construction contracts: NEC4 introduced a core clause 29 "Disclosure" that "restricts the contractor's ability to publicise the works without the client's agreement", the NEC4 subcontract's clause 29.1 prohibits disclosure of information except as needed to perform the contract, and UK guidance on subcontractor agreements says showing completed work in a portfolio "can be completely normal but should be agreed upfront" ([C-Link, NEC3 vs NEC4](https://c-link.com/blog/nec3-vs-nec4/); [NEC on IP in the ECS](https://www.neccontract.com/news/how-the-nec4-ecs-deals-with-intellectual-property-rights); [Sprintlaw, subcontractor agreements](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/subcontractor-agreement-in-the-uk-key-terms-to-include/)). The researchers could not access the JCT sub-contract text, so the founders must read their own agreement, checking for confidentiality, publicity and photography clauses, non-solicitation of clients or staff, any non-compete, and IP assignment. Non-solicitation clauses of "commonly 3–12 months" restricted to clients the person actually worked with are plausibly enforceable; blanket non-competes "are harder to enforce in the UK and must be tightly drafted to avoid being void as a 'restraint of trade'"; general marketing (website, SEO, ads) is normally not "solicitation", but directly approaching the main contractor's end clients could be, and enforcement by injunction typically costs £10,000-plus, which cuts both ways ([Sprintlaw, non-solicitation](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/understanding-non-solicitation-clauses-in-uk-business-contracts-a-guide-for-employers-and-entrepreneurs/); [Hybrid Legal](https://www.hybridlegal.co.uk/blog/restrictive-covenants-non-solicitation-clauses-a-reality-check-for-uk-businesses)). All of this is marked "solicitor to confirm" in the notes.

The permission request itself should name the specific projects, photos and figures to be used, offer neutral wording, commit not to name end clients without separate consent, commit to remove any item on request, and ask for a reference contact. The researchers' skeleton (their own drafting, not a sourced template) reads: "Dear [MC], as you know [we] are setting up [NewCo]. We would like to reference, on our website, the commercial PV work we carried out for you as subcontractors between [dates], using the aggregate figures [X MWp / Y sites], the attached [n] photographs (no client names, signage or people shown), and the wording: '[proposed sentence]'. We will not name [MC] or its clients unless you confirm you are happy for us to do so, and we will remove any item at your request. Would you be willing to confirm this in writing, and could [site manager] provide a short reference on our workmanship?" Testimonials from the main contractor's site managers are permissible if the person consents and the quote is genuine and held on file with contact details, so ask for them in a personal capacity and label them that way; quotes from the main contractor's end clients need both the client's and, practically, the main contractor's permission, and without both should not be used ([ASA/CAP testimonials](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/testimonials-and-endorsements.html)).

Photo rights follow a similar logic. "The author or creator of the work is usually the first owner" of copyright, works created "in the course of his employment" belong to the employer, and commissioned works belong to the creator unless assigned in writing, so photographs the brothers took themselves while working probably belong to them or their company ([GOV.UK, copyright ownership](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/ownership-of-copyright-works)). Ownership, though, does not override confidentiality or publicity clauses, site photography rules or UK GDPR: identifiable people are personal data, "client files visible on a desk" can be a breach of confidence, and businesses are advised to use written consent forms, check backgrounds for screens, documents and addresses, and keep a takedown procedure ([Sprintlaw, posting photos](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/uk-laws-on-posting-photos-without-consent-for-businesses/)). The practical rule is to publish only photos that the founders took, that show only their own work, that contain no identifiable people, signage, site names or documents, and that the main contractor has cleared in writing; client-owned drone shots, the main contractor's marketing images and anything from its monitoring portal are off limits. A contractor case-study checklist adds two habits worth adopting: record for every asset who created it and the rights basis, and "Do not imply responsibility for an entire renovation when the contractor completed one trade" ([Nexxen checklist](https://nexxensolutions.ca/resources/contractor-project-photos-case-study-permission-checklist)).

The existing electrical business is a genuine asset if it is labelled honestly. Murray Electrical's reviews can appear under a clearly headed "Our electrical contracting track record" block, but must not be merged into a solar reviews carousel, because a testimonial must relate to the product or service advertised ([ASA/CAP testimonials](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/testimonials-and-endorsements.html)). If the solar company genuinely is the same business, the Google Business Profile should be renamed rather than recreated, because reviews are tied to the profile and creating a new listing loses them; if it is a different business, a new profile is the honest route ([Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3098204?hl=en)). LinkedIn recommendations on the founders' personal profiles are naturally personal, publicly verifiable, and can be linked from the About page, which matters because "Over 90% of respondents said they trust peers in their industry more than vendor content" ([Trustmary](https://trustmary.com/social-proof/how-trust-elements-impact-b2b-buying-decisions/)).

### Credibility substitutes, ranked by impact and ease

For a commercial-only firm the credibility stack UK buyers actually check is, in order, electrical registration, MCS, insurance certificates, an SSIP health-and-safety badge, working-at-height cards, an insurance-backed workmanship guarantee and manufacturer certifications; consumer codes and trade directories are domestic signals. A 2026 buyer guide expects "£5m+ public liability" and "£5m+ professional indemnity insurance", "IPAF + PASMA tickets" and "References from at least 3 commercial installs of similar size in the last 18 months", and calls the insurance-backed warranty "the one that matters most for commercial buyers" because it protects the client if the installer fails during a 10-year workmanship warranty ([Solar Panels for Businesses, installers 2026](https://solarpanelsforbusinesses.co.uk/commercial-solar-installers-uk/); [Solar Panels for Businesses, how to choose](https://solarpanelsforbusinesses.co.uk/blog/how-to-choose-commercial-solar-installer-uk-business/)). MCS deserves its own note. Its technical scope is design, installation and commissioning of PV "up to a maximum DC output of 50kWp", so most large commercial roofs fall outside it, yet buyer guides still treat MCS as a walk-away criterion and a PPA provider directory lists "MCS and NICEIC accreditation" among EPC requirements ([MIS 3002:2025](https://mcscertified.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/MIS-3002-2025-V1.0.pdf); [solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk providers](https://solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk/providers/)). Certification requires three roles (Main Contact, Licensee, Technical Supervisor), £2m public liability and professional indemnity, and "at least one fully compliant installation" for assessment; the realistic timeline for an already-qualified electrician with an installation ready is "2–4 months", and NICEIC's 2026/27 first-year fees total £1,044 including VAT ([MCS, becoming certified](https://mcscertified.com/installers/becoming-certified/); [Solarable, MCS timeline](https://solarable.org/guides/mcs-certification-timeline-small-installer); [NICEIC MCS scheme](https://niceic.com/for-the-trades-1/professional-standards/schemes/mcs-scheme/)). The assessment install "cannot be done retrospectively", which is why the first sub-50 kWp job and the first case study should be the same project ([Solarable, MCS timeline](https://solarable.org/guides/mcs-certification-timeline-small-installer)).

### Imagery without your own project photos

Every design source agrees that real photography beats stock, and UK critics add that even real drone-roof shots fail when they show "No context. No people. No emotion" ([Mouthy Marketing](https://www.mouthymarketing.co.uk/marketing-for-solar-companies-is-broken-heres-how-to-fix-it/)). The layered plan is: first, a half-day branded shoot of the founders (£200–£800 is the typical UK add-on cost) covering headshots, the van, workwear, tools, PPE, measurement and planning detail, and "working images of them in various ways", staged at a training centre, a distributor's demo rig or a friendly farm or warehouse roof with permission, and captioned "our team" and "our kit", never "our recent installation" ([Jo Scott Images](https://www.joscottimages.co.uk/blog/what-can-be-covered-in-branding-photography-for-the-trades/); [Whito](https://whito.co.uk/research/website-design-costs-uk/)). Second, manufacturer and partner assets whose terms expressly allow marketing use: the SolarEdge partner portal offers logos, product photos and videos to registered installers, K2 Systems publishes mounting-system images for tiled, flat and trapezoidal roofs on condition of the credit "K2 Systems GmbH", and SMA licenses its logo for website use once its terms are accepted and a trademark footnote attached; LONGi, Fronius and others describe press assets as editorial or restricted, so ask their UK reps for written marketing permission and keep the emails ([SolarEdge partner portal](https://marketing.solaredge.com/partners-portal-hq/brand); [K2 press and media](https://k2-systems.com/en/company/press-media/); [SMA logo usage](https://policy.sma.energy/en/logo-usage)). Third, paid stock for hero rooftop and industrial shots, at Adobe Stock's £19.99 a month for 10 assets or Envato Elements at $16.50 a month if its themes are also used; free stock (Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay) only for secondary imagery, since none guarantees model releases and identifiable people or branded kit create risk ([Adobe Stock UK](https://stock.adobe.com/uk/plans); [Envato Elements pricing](https://elements.envato.com/pricing); [Pixabay licence](https://pixabay.com/service/license-summary/)). Fourth, drone footage licensed from Pond5 (clips from $15–$39) or Artgrid ($19.99 a month), or flown by the founders under a Flyer ID and an £11.13-a-year Operator ID with A2 CofC training and aviation liability insurance ([Pond5](https://www.pond5.com/search?kw=aerial-solar&media=footage); [Coptrz, UK drone laws 2026](https://coptrz.com/blog/british-drone-laws-2026-a-guide-to-uk-drone-licencing/)). Renders and AI images may appear only as labelled concept visuals ("illustrative render of a 200 kWp flat-roof array"), never in a project context, because the CAP Code is "media-neutral" and "disclosure alone is very unlikely to mitigate the harm caused by a fundamentally misleading message" ([ASA/CAP, AI disclosure](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/disclosure-of-ai-in-advertising-striking-the-balance-between-creativity-and-responsibility.html)). A written content-provenance log (source, licence, date, file) is cheap insurance against both licensing demands and ASA challenges. After the first two or three installs, real project photography should replace the stock hero.

### The 90-day plan to the first three case studies

Three well-documented small projects beat a vague experience claim: contractor-portfolio guidance holds that "Three well-documented small projects are more convincing than a vague claim of '10 years of experience'", each written as situation, complication, solution and outcome in 200–300 words with a testimonial tied to the project ([TaskTag](https://blog.tasktag.com/portfolio-ideas-for-general-contractors)). UK commercial case studies typically state kWp and panel count, annual generation and specific yield, first-year and lifetime savings, payback, self-sufficiency, CO2 saved, kit, roof and orientation, and a new installer's site shows the bar with "294 kW system, 560 panels, 4.5-year payback, £1.2m savings projection" ([Generate Solar EV](https://generate-solarev.co.uk/)). The plan assumes the founders can keep subcontracting in parallel and that a solicitor has cleared the subcontract terms.

## Build it on Webflow with Energy X, or on WordPress with a renewables freelancer

Platform is not what separates good commercial solar sites from bad ones: Photon Energy (Squarespace) and Eden Sustainable (Wix) read as trustworthy as agency WordPress builds, and Fort Energy (Wix) and VCT Group (Squarespace) present better than several Elementor sites with broken counters ([Photon Energy](https://www.photonenergy.co.uk/); [Eden Sustainable](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/); [Fort Energy](https://www.fortenergy.ie/); [VCT Group](https://vctgroup.com/commercial-solar)). WordPress dominates the sector, roughly two-thirds of the UK sites fetched and 11 of 15 reachable UK commercial solar sites checked by hand, and it is what UK renewables specialists build on, so any local agency quote will almost certainly be WordPress; a plugin-heavy WordPress site, though, "can become slow and fragile" and "Commercial WordPress themes inject bloat reducing speed" ([Podium Design](https://www.podiumdesign.co.uk/services/renewable-energy-website-design); [Zenith Design platform comparison](https://zenithdesign.studio/blog/website-platform-comparison); [DemandConvert](https://demandconvert.com/learn/blog/solar-website-design-examples/)). Business email adds about £5–£6 per user per month on Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace ([Compare the Cloud](https://www.comparethecloud.net/articles/google-workspace-vs-microsoft-365-business-uk-startup-5-20-staff-all-in-gbp-costs)). Putting that together, realistic all-in first-year budgets are roughly **£500–£900** for pure DIY on Webflow or Framer with a template, stock subscription and photoshoot; **£1,500–£3,500** for freelancer-assisted setup of the same template; **£1,500–£5,000** plus £120–£400 a year hosting and care for WordPress through a renewables freelancer; and **£4,000–£8,000** for a local agency. Whichever route is chosen, allow one to two weeks to write about ten pages of original copy and a day for the founder photoshoot before anything can launch; a template build is then days for DIY or two to four weeks with a freelancer. Fiverr and Upwork gigs suit bounded tasks such as template customisation, a calculator embed or a logo rather than the whole site ([Upwork hourly rates](https://www.upwork.com/resources/upwork-hourly-rates)).

### Compliance pages a UK limited company site must carry

The legal minimum is more than a privacy policy. Regulation 25 of the Company, LLP and Business (Names and Trading Disclosures) Regulations 2015 requires every company to disclose on its websites "the part of the United Kingdom in which the company is registered", "the company's registered number" and "the address of the company's registered office", with the registered name also required, and failure "without reasonable excuse" is an offence by the company and every officer in default, punishable by a level 3 fine plus a daily default fine ([SI 2015/17, Part 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/17/part/6); [ICAS reminder](https://www.icas.com/news-insights-events/news/practice/business-names-and-other-disclosures-a-reminder)). The E-Commerce Regulations 2002 add the geographic address, email address, trade-register details and "the VAT number" where VAT-registered, with any prices stated "clearly and unambiguously"; the Provision of Services Regulations 2009 add "legal status and form", "General terms, conditions, and contractual provisions regarding jurisdiction" and "Professional liability insurance or guarantee details" ([SI 2002/2013, reg 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2013/regulation/6); [SI 2009/2999, reg 8](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2009/2999/regulation/8)). The privacy notice must follow the ICO's list: who you are, each purpose and lawful basis, who data is shared with, retention periods, individual rights, how to withdraw consent and the right to complain to the ICO, which for this site means covering enquiry forms, the CRM processor (HubSpot or Pipedrive) and analytics ([ICO, privacy information](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/the-right-to-be-informed/what-privacy-information-should-we-provide/)). Cookie handling changed in 2026: the ICO's guidance on storage and access technologies, finalised on 29 April 2026, requires consent mechanisms to "provide equal prominence for rejection and acceptance" and bans pre-enabling non-exempt technologies, while new "Statistical purposes" and "Appearance" exceptions let analytics and preference cookies run without consent if users get "a simple means of objecting"; the amended PECR rules came into force on 5 February 2026 and fines are now aligned with UK GDPR at "£17,500,000 or 4% of an undertaking's total worldwide annual turnover" ([ICO, storage and access technologies](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-the-use-of-storage-and-access-technologies/); [Womble Bond Dickinson](https://www.womblebonddickinson.com/uk/insights/articles-and-briefings/new-exceptions-non-intrusive-cookies-icos-updated-guidance-has)). The Equality Act 2010 applies to private businesses, "An inaccessible website can constitute a failure to make reasonable adjustments", and WCAG 2.2 AA is the recognised benchmark ([Level Access](https://www.levelaccess.com/blog/website-accessibility-laws-in-the-u-k/)). The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 "prohibits fabricated endorsements and requires verification of testimonials" ([HWB Accountants](https://www.hwb-accountants.com/news-guides/news/legal-requirements-for-company-websites-in-the-uk-2026-guide/)).

The minimum set, then, is a footer line on every page ("[Company] Ltd is registered in England and Wales, company number X, registered office [address], VAT number Y"), a contact page with geographic address, email and phone, a privacy notice, a cookie banner with equal-weight Accept and Reject and an analytics toggle, terms of website use plus a service-information page listing legal status, insurer and general terms, an accessibility statement targeting WCAG 2.2 AA, and, once a consumer code applies, a complaints route. Scheme logos have their own rules: the MCS Certified Mark may be used only while certified, only for certified services, reproduced from artwork issued by the certification body, never "redrawn, recoloured, stretched, squeezed or distorted", and MCS "will take action against any misuse"; the RECC logo may be used only by code members, must link to the RECC website, must not be grouped "with the logos of manufacturers", and claiming code membership falsely is an offence under the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 ([MCS brand guidelines](https://www.szutest.cz/www/upload/categories/documents/20200210015603128.pdf); [MCS, marketing with MCS](https://mcscertified.com/installers/becoming-certified/use-mcs-to-market-your-business-welcome-to-mcs/); [RECC logo guidance](https://www.recc.org.uk/pdf/guidance-on-use-of-the-logo.pdf)). Manufacturer logos should appear only where the firm holds the status implied and, for SMA, only after its licence terms are accepted and the trademark footnote added ([SMA logo usage](https://policy.sma.energy/en/logo-usage)).

## Assumptions, unfetched sites and points a solicitor must confirm

This report assumes the business is UK-based: the founders' existing company, Murray Electrical, the accreditation vocabulary (NICEIC, MCS, CHAS, RECC), the funders, the tax reliefs and the advertising and company-law rules are all UK-specific, and the international sites were used for design and structure inspiration only. A reader elsewhere should swap the accreditation stack for the local equivalent (Clean Energy Council, NETCC and state licence numbers in Australia; Safe Electric and SEAI in Ireland; state contractor licences such as a CSLB number in the US), replace the UK PPA regulatory position (the funder relying on the automatic Ofgem Class A supply exemption) and the UK tax treatment (AIA and the 50% special-rate first-year allowance) with local rules such as the US 30% investment tax credit or Canada's Class 43.2 accelerated CCA, and re-check the copying analysis, because US courts have protected the "look and feel" of a website as trade dress in a way UK courts have not ([Select Electrical](https://selectelectricalonline.com.au/); [Solar X financing](https://solar-x.ca/commercial/solar-financing); [Mondaq, Ingrid & Isabel](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/advertising-marketing-branding/347268/look-and-feel-of-a-website-can-constitute-protectable-trade-dress)). The funder shortlist is UK-only; the Irish framing of PPAs ("no upfront cost, we maintain it, gifted at the end") is the most transferable to the UK, whereas Australian "three finance cards" and US "customer-owned vs investor-owned" vocabularies need translating ([AEI PPA](https://aei.ie/commercial/power-purchase-agreement-ppa/); [SunPeak financing](https://www.sunpeakpower.com/solar-financing)).

The researchers flagged several gaps that qualify the rankings. Every site was judged from extracted text, fonts and inline colour counts rather than rendered screenshots, so animation, spacing and mobile behaviour were not assessed, and the international researcher recommends "a visual review of the top 8 on desktop and mobile" before a final choice ([Todae Solar](https://www.todaesolar.com.au/)). Several plausible UK models could not be fetched on 28 September 2026: Solarsense and Absolute Solar & Wind served a SiteGround robot-challenge page, Zestec, Mypower (an award-winning Gloucestershire commercial installer) and HDM Energies returned HTTP 503, Sundog Energy returned 522 and Deege Solar a WordPress error page, so the shortlist may omit strong regional sites ([Solarsense PPA page](https://solarsense-uk.com/commercial-renewable-energy-for-businesses/solar-power-purchase-agreement/)). Internationally, Thomas Electrical (Australia), Greentricity and Sun Arc Solar (Ireland), DSD Renewables and SkyFire Energy were bot-blocked or JavaScript-only, and the block structures of the UK sites praised in agency roundups (Solarsense, Solar For Business, DB Energy) could not be extracted ([Zixel UK examples](https://www.zixel.co.uk/web-design-examples/solar-website-examples/)). No conversion or traffic data was available for any site, so all rankings are qualitative, and the conversion statistics in the design notes come largely from agencies that sell solar websites and from residential or cross-industry studies, not from UK commercial solar sites; the "dedicated pages convert 5×" and form-field figures should be read as directional ([Web Tonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/solar-energy-landing-page-statistics)).

Several legal and commercial points need a solicitor or a direct enquiry. The subcontract itself was not available, no source quotes a JCT standard-form publicity clause, and no UK judgment exists squarely on website look and feel, so the wording guide, the permission letter and the copying table are general information. The claim that MCS now audits installers' online presence for misleading claims rests on a single secondary source and could not be checked against the MCS operating requirements, which returned a 404 ([Solarable, MCS timeline](https://solarable.org/guides/mcs-certification-timeline-small-installer)). Whether insurance-backed guarantee providers will underwrite a new company's first non-domestic jobs, and what professional-indemnity limits PPA funders demand, could not be confirmed; no funder publishes a full term sheet (buy-out formula, termination charges, performance guarantees), so those must be obtained under NDA. The PPA rate ranges quoted are marketing-derived, two of the pricing sources are SEO sites operated by the same company, and the "business rates exemption until 2035" claim on Geo Green Power's finance page could not be verified against GOV.UK. Finlight's identity as the renamed Atrato business is inferred from a redirect and Brookfield's stated plan rather than confirmed by a press release, and INICIO, Solar Nation, Balance Power and OnStream Solar could not be verified as active rooftop-PPA funders. Finally, the plan assumes the founders are already NICEIC or NAPIT registered through Murray Electrical and can extend that registration to the new company, and that the subcontract contains no enforceable non-compete covering solar work; neither was verifiable and both should be settled before launch.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the brothers' problem. They asked which site to copy and how to hide the lack of a portfolio; the evidence says the portfolio is not what makes the best small-firm sites persuasive in the first place. Excel Energy, Ecoaim, Select Electrical and Fort Energy all rank at the top of the copyability scale precisely because their credibility comes from process transparency, explained accreditations, named funders and honest numbers, and they push only a handful of projects. A site built on that pattern, with the founders' subcontract experience stated plainly and quantified (something no UK new entrant in the sample does), will out-present the cautionary examples that have more projects but broken counters, unlabelled logo walls or PPA pages without terms. The single most useful sequencing insight is that the first sub-50 kWp install, the MCS assessment and case study one are the same job, which collapses the "no proof" phase to about six weeks if the founders' own premises or a friendly SME can host it.

Two implications follow for the months after launch. First, the PPA page is a lead qualifier rather than a sales page: its job is to send 100 kWp-plus, daytime-load, long-tenure businesses to a suitability form and everyone else to the capital-purchase page, and it can only name a funder once an accreditation with Smart Ease, UrbanVolt or Electron Green exists, which itself depends on MCS, insurance and the first references. Second, the site should be treated as two releases, an interim version that leans on wording, process, badges and modelled examples, and a proof-led version after day 90 that demotes the example systems behind real case studies and adds the MCS number, manufacturer certifications and reviews. The cheapest mistakes to avoid are behavioural rather than technical: slow follow-up, borrowed or unlabelled imagery, unsourced savings claims and any sentence that lets a reader think the main contractor's projects were the new company's own.
