# UK commercial rooftop solar PPAs: how a small installer can offer "funded solar" (research notes, 28 September 2026)

Scope note: UK focus, current as at September 2026. Older figures are dated inline. "Typical" means it appears across several independent sources; "negotiated" means sources say it varies deal by deal. Several aggregator/SEO sites (e.g. solarpowerpurchaseagreements.co.uk and commercialsolarpanelsinstallation.co.uk, both operated by SEO Dons Ltd) are cited only for indicative figures and are flagged as such. ## Key question 1: How a UK rooftop solar PPA works for a business customer, and typical eligibility

### Takeaway
A UK rooftop PPA is a long-term (usually 15–25 year, most often 25-year) contract under which a third-party funder pays for, owns, insures and maintains a solar array on the customer's roof and sells the customer the electricity it generates at a fixed p/kWh price (2026 indicative range roughly 9–18p/kWh for typical rooftop sizes, usually with a 2–3% or RPI/CPI annual escalator), with the customer able to buy, extend or have the system removed at the end of the term; eligibility in practice hinges on a minimum system of about 100 kWp (some funders go down to 50–70 kW), consistent daytime load, a freehold or long remaining lease, and a creditworthy off-taker.

### Cited Findings

Structure and ownership
- "A PPA investor funds the design and installation of the system and retains ownership for the duration of the agreement"; the site operator "purchases the electricity generated at a fixed price per kWh" that is "lower than the current grid electricity rate"; the investor receives export income from surplus power sold to the grid — [Spirit Energy](https://www.spiritenergy.co.uk/ppa-solar)
- Solar Energy UK (trade body) defines a PPA as a contract where the producer installs a solar system at minimal cost and the customer receives reduced electricity rates; contracts "typically run 10-25 years" with the developer managing O&M; at the end the customer may remove the system, extend the agreement, or purchase it. It lists five prerequisites: sufficient electricity demand, suitable location (rule of thumb minimum 1 m² per kW of roof), planning permission for installations over 1 MW, grid connection availability, and customer creditworthiness — [Solar Energy UK](https://solarenergyuk.org/resource/power-purchase-agreements/)
- The funder side: Two Blues Solar "owns the installation for the duration of the PPA, leasing or sub-leasing the roof space from the customer during this period"; at the end of the term ownership transfers to the building owner; terms "between 10 years and 25 years" — [Photon Energy](https://www.photonenergy.co.uk/power-purchase-agreement)
- eEnergy (public-sector focused): "You lease your roof space to eEnergy and we own, operate, maintain and manage the system", with an "Option to own the solar system at the end of the agreement" — [eEnergy](https://www.eenergy.com/funding-with-a-power-purchase-agreement-ppa/)

- Eden: provider covers installation, inverter replacement, cleaning, inspections, monitoring, insurance — [Eden Sustainable](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa); MTFD: "Monitoring, maintenance, repair and insurance all sit with the funder for the term" — [MTFD](https://mtfd.co.uk/zero-capex-solar); Solar4Business: "We handle all monitoring, maintenance, and insurance for the 25-year life of the system at no extra cost" — [Solar4Business](https://solar4business.uk/commercial-solar-ppa/); Carbon3 warns cleaning may or may not be included: "panel cleaning — but check your contract!" — [Carbon3](https://carbon3.co.uk/carbon3-guide-to-ppa-what-is-a-power-purchase-agreement-uk/)

End of term and buyout
- Eden: three options — "Take ownership. You can buy the system at a nominal end-of-term value", "Extend the agreement", or "Have the system removed. We remove all equipment and reinstate the roof at no cost to you"; systems retain approximately 88% output after 25 years — [Eden Sustainable](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa)
- Ecoaim: "(1) buy the system for a pre-agreed nominal residual value, (2) extend the agreement, or (3) have it removed" — [Ecoaim](https://ecoaim.co.uk/blog/funding-solar-with-ppa-uk/)
- Several providers say ownership simply transfers at the end: "the asset is then gifted to the client" — [AR Power](https://www.arpower.co.uk/funding-options/power-purchase-agreement/); "ownership of the system transfers to the lessee" — [AB Renewables](https://ab-renewables.co.uk/ppa-funding/); "gifted" after 25 years — [3S Northampton / Electron Green](https://3sltd.co.uk/ppa/); "At the end of the contract, you gain full ownership of the Solar PV array" — [Centrica Business Solutions](https://www.centricabusinesssolutions.com/blogpost/kick-start-your-net-zero-pathway-solar-ppa)

Early termination, sale of premises, relocation
- Sprintlaw (14 May 2026): "The PPA should state whether it can be assigned, novated or transferred to a new occupier or owner"; early termination charges may be calculated "by reference to the provider's lost revenue over the remaining term, which may make termination expensive"; recommends reviewing lease documents before engaging providers, modelling full-term pricing not just year-one rates, and clarifying end-of-term removal and reinstatement costs — [Sprintlaw](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/power-purchase-agreements-for-solar-installations-in-the-uk/)
- Eden FAQ: "If your business relocates and the new occupant of the building is willing to take on the PPA terms, the agreement can transfer to them. If no suitable transferee is available, the contract includes provisions for early termination." — [Eden Sustainable](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa)
- Walker Morris (Aug 2022): "Only the parties to the PPA are bound by it. Unlike lease terms, the PPA won't 'pass' to a successor"; landlord's consent "will usually be required for making any exterior alterations" — [Walker Morris](https://www.walkermorris.co.uk/comment-opinion/rooftop-solar-a-win-win/)
- Stephens Scown: a lease "creates a legal interest" that persists through property sales, whereas a licence is contractual permission that "cannot be assigned to a third party"; leases require Land Registry registration, licences are "exempt from Stamp Duty Land Tax and registration at HM Land Registry"; a PPA can be standalone or incorporated into the lease, and combining them "create[s] inseparability issues if the property is sold" — [Stephens Scown](https://www.stephens-scown.co.uk/specialist-sectors/renewable-energy/which-is-better-a-solar-lease-licence-or-ppa/)
- Naked Solar markets its PPA as "Fully transferable" — [Naked Solar](https://nakedsolar.co.uk/solar-pv/ppa-business/)

Landlord / tenant / leased buildings
- Eden (Nov 2024, updated Sept 2025): "Installing solar panels on leased properties typically requires explicit permission from the building owner"; documents needed include a "License to Alter" and, if subleasing, a "License to Underlet"; agreements should address "lease renewals, property sales, and potential impacts on property value" and "what happens to the solar panels when the lease ends"; also planning, building regs, DNO approval and supply licence exemption — [Eden Sustainable](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/news/can-commercial-solar-panels-be-installed-on-leased-buildings)
- Sprintlaw: consent "may be needed for alterations, roof use, third party access and the grant of rights"; a common mistake is agreeing heads of terms with the solar provider before reviewing the lease — [Sprintlaw](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/power-purchase-agreements-for-solar-installations-in-the-uk/)
- Landlord-as-generator model: a landlord can install, own and sell power to its occupier under a PPA and monetise REGOs; tenants get price certainty at rates "likely to be lower than purchasing from the electricity network" — [Walker Morris](https://www.walkermorris.co.uk/comment-opinion/rooftop-solar-a-win-win/)

Balance sheet / accounting
- Eden: "A PPA is classified as an operating expense, not a capital purchase. There is no asset on your balance sheet, no depreciation charge, and no debt"; it argues the contract doesn't qualify as a lease under IFRS 16 because "you are buying electricity, not securing the right to use an identified asset" — [Eden Sustainable](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa)
- GreenHearth (finance broker guide): PPA payments "treated as operating expenses"; "Off-balance sheet treatment possible"; suits larger projects "(250+ MWh/year consumption)" — [GreenHearth](https://www.greenhearth.energy/guide-to-financing-commercial-solar)
- MD Govier advises consulting accountants because of IFRS 16 implications — [MD Govier](https://www.govier-electrical-engineering.co.uk/blog/commercial-solar-financing-uk/)

### Inferences
- Typical practice: 25-year term, fixed year-1 p/kWh with 2–3% or RPI/CPI escalator, funder-borne O&M/insurance/monitoring, three end-of-term options (buy at nominal value, extend, free removal). Negotiated case by case: the actual p/kWh (driven by size, self-consumption, credit strength, term, escalator), buy-out mechanics, assignment/early-termination terms, cleaning inclusion, roof lease vs licence.
- Public "discount vs grid" claims range from 10% to 50%; the honest framing for a website is that the rate is set at a discount to the customer's current grid price and the size of the discount depends on the site. Year-1 rate comparisons flatter the deal if the escalator is ignored (see the aggregator's worked example of +£245k–£316k over 20 years).
- The de facto entry threshold for third-party funders is ~100 kWp / ~80–100 MWh a year of load. Smart Ease (70 kW) and the SEO site's 50 kWp are the low end; big funders (Centrica at 1 MW+, per its LinkedIn description in KQ2) sit far above a small installer's typical project.
- Because a PPA does not automatically bind a successor, any funded deal needs a roof lease (registrable, survives sale) or at least a licence plus assignment/novation provisions, and landlord consent on leased sites. This is why funders ask for freehold or 15+ years of remaining lease.

### Gaps
- No funder publishes a full term sheet online (buy-out formula, termination charge formula, performance guarantees, roof reinstatement obligations); these must be obtained from funders under NDA.
- No primary evidence on how often escalators are RPI vs CPI vs fixed in 2026 deals; only marketing statements.
- The Green Shield Group eligibility guide (search snippet: minimum 10-year remaining lease, well-established business) could not be fetched, so its specifics are unverified.

## Key question 2: Which UK funders/asset owners currently offer fully funded rooftop PPAs and partner with installers/EPCs (checked list)

### Takeaway
The realistic rooftop-PPA funders for a small installer's pipeline in September 2026 are the specialist C&I platforms — AMPYR Distributed Energy (and its owned/partnered developers Eden Sustainable, Shawton, Olympus Power, Harvest Green), Zestec (Octopus-owned) via the Smart Ease installer channel, Smart Ease itself, Electron Green, UrbanVolt, Two Blues Solar, the Brookfield-backed ex-Atrato business (now apparently branded Finlight), NZFM and Wattstor — while most of the names on the original list (Innova, ILOS, Enviromena, Aura Power, Bluefield, Low Carbon, Elmya, Drax, BayWa r.e., Kimmeridge) are utility-scale or corporate-PPA businesses that do not fund sub-1 MW rooftops, and several others (Brite Solar, Clean Energy Capital, "Solar for Business", Gridserve, Energy Estates, Sunstone, Novus, Balance Power) either could not be verified or are not rooftop-PPA funders.

## Key question 3: How installer–funder partnerships work commercially, what funders require, and how realistic it is for a brand-new two-person firm

### Takeaway
The standard model is that the installer originates and designs the project and is paid as EPC contractor for the build (often in stage payments, sometimes trading margin for a slice of PPA revenue), the funder owns the asset and holds the PPA, and O&M is either subcontracted back to the installer or done by the funder; published partner criteria centre on Tier-1 equipment, HSE compliance, transparent staged pricing and a creditworthy customer, and platforms like Smart Ease onboard installers through an accreditation-and-training process, so a new two-person firm can realistically act as an accredited channel partner/introducer on 70–500 kWp projects but should expect to subcontract or joint-venture the build on larger jobs.

### Inferences
- Commercial reality (inferred from the above; no funder publishes numbers): the installer's income on a funded job is its EPC contract price (design + supply + install, paid in stages), sometimes plus an O&M contract for the funder, and optionally a revenue share in lieu of margin (UrbanVolt). Pure introducer fees exist but are undisclosed; a new firm should ask each funder for its introducer terms in writing.
- For a two-person firm: MCS is not needed above 50 kW, but funders will still want evidence of competence (NICEIC/NAPIT electrical registration, G99 experience, Tier-1 equipment with bankable warranties, public/product/employer's liability insurance, CDM competence and a method for working at height). Because none of that is published as a checklist, the safest route is to (1) get accredited with Smart Ease and register with UrbanVolt's partner programme as a sales/design partner, (2) approach Electron Green, Eden/Shawton (ADE) and Two Blues Solar as an introducer/subcontract installer, and (3) partner with an established EPC (e.g. Custom Solar for Zestec work, or any regional EPC) to co-deliver the first 250 kWp+ projects while building a track record.
- Customer relationship: in every published model the installer keeps the customer-facing role (UrbanVolt explicitly), but the customer signs the PPA and roof lease with the funder's SPV, so the installer must be honest on its website that "our funding partner" owns the system and sets the final rate.

- No public source gives EPC margin ranges, referral fee percentages, or the split of O&M revenue; these are negotiated and confidential.
- No published funder due-diligence checklist for installers (insurance limits, warranty terms, financial standing); UrbanVolt's list is the closest.
- Solar Nation's website could not be fetched; the £380m funding claim is the installer's own statement.

## Key question 4: Alternative funding routes to present alongside a PPA, and how sites compare them

### Takeaway
Installers that present PPAs well always show them next to outright purchase (with AIA and the 50% special-rate first-year allowance — not "full expensing", which excludes solar panels), asset finance/hire purchase or leasing over roughly 5–10 years via FCA-regulated brokers (installers typically act as introducer appointed representatives), and green business loans; the standard comparison axes are ownership, upfront cost, who claims tax relief, who carries maintenance, contract length and lifetime return, with the consensus that self-funding gives the highest lifetime return while a PPA gives day-one savings with no capital or operating risk.

### Cited Findings

Tax treatment (UK companies, 2026)
- HMRC HS252: "Full expensing enables companies within the charge to corporation tax to claim 100% of the cost of main rate plant and machinery"; "Companies within the charge to corporation tax can claim 50% of the cost of special rate plant and machinery"; "Special rate assets include integral features of buildings and structures, solar panels and assets with an expected life of at least 25 years"; AIA limit £1,000,000; WDA rates "main pool rate — 18%; special pool rate — 6%" (the helpsheet predates the 2026 main-pool change) — [HMRC HS252](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/capital-allowances-and-balancing-charges-hs252-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs252-capital-allowances-and-balancing-charges-2024)
- Finance Act 2024 s.1 removed the words "but before 1 April 2026", making full expensing permanent — [legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/3/section/1); a tax-services guide states the 50% special-rate FYA's April 2026 end date was also removed and that "Solar panels are special rate assets, so full expensing and the 40% first-year allowance don't cover them" — [Tax Rebate Services](https://www.taxrebateservices.co.uk/business-solar-tax-relief-2026/)
- New 40% first-year allowance for main-rate expenditure from 1 January 2026 (available to unincorporated businesses and leased assets, excluding second-hand assets and cars); main pool WDA cut from 18% to 14% from 1 April 2026 (CT) / 6 April 2026 (IT); "This measure does not change the WDA on the special rate pool which is currently 6%" — [GOV.UK policy paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/new-first-year-allowance-and-main-rate-of-writing-down-allowances/capital-allowances-new-first-year-allowance-and-reducing-main-rate-writing-down-allowances)
- Installers' own tax statements: Excel Energy correctly states solar panels qualify for the "50% special rate first-year allowance (not full expensing)" and that "tax benefits may sit with the funder, not the site occupier" in lease/PPA structures — [Excel Energy](https://excelenergy.co.uk/news/how-to-finance-commercial-solar-panels-in-the-uk/); Geo Green Power: AIA "Deduct 100% of the cost of your solar installation (up to £1 million) from pre-tax profits in the first year", 50% FYA "for multi-million-pound projects", and a claim that "most new rooftop solar installations are exempt from business rates until 2035" — [Geo Green Power](https://www.geogreenpower.com/finance-options-for-solar-projects-commercial-funding-and-solar-business-grants/); Eden states "CAPEX also qualifies for full expensing capital allowances under current HMRC rules" — [Eden Sustainable](https://www.edensustainable.co.uk/solar-ppa) — contradicted by HS252's classification of solar panels as special rate (50% FYA), although AIA gives 100% relief up to £1m.
- PPA tax point: the customer "cannot claim Annual Investment Allowance (AIA); the PPA provider retains this benefit and asset ownership" — [commercialsolarpanelsinstallation.co.uk (SEO Dons Ltd)](https://commercialsolarpanelsinstallation.co.uk/commercial-solar-panel-financing/)

## Key question 5: Regulatory and advertising considerations

### Takeaway
A behind-the-meter rooftop PPA is an electricity supply contract, not a credit agreement, so it is outside FCA regulation (installers that also introduce asset finance need credit-broking cover, and the Consumer Duty only bites on FCA-regulated firms' retail customers); the funder relies on the automatic Class A "small supplier" exemption (≤5 MW, self-generated output only, no registration, but statutory duties under Schedule 2ZB and a contract required) or on the Class C on-site exemption; MCS and the consumer codes (RECC/HIES) are domestic-scale/consumer schemes that do not apply to B2B rooftop work above 50 kW; and B2B advertising is covered by the CAP Code and the BPRs 2008, with 2025–26 ASA rulings making clear that "up to X%" savings, "free" and "£0" claims must be substantiated and qualified in the ad itself.

### Cited Findings

Ofgem / DESNZ licensing
- DESNZ FAQ (July 2017, revised September 2026): section 4(1) Electricity Act 1989 makes it an offence to generate for supply, distribute or supply electricity to premises without a licence or exemption; class exemptions "apply automatically" and "an undertaking does not need to apply to the Department or to Ofgem for the exemption to apply, or to notify that they are claiming a class exemption"; Class A: "Persons (other than licensed suppliers) who do not supply any electricity except electricity which they generate themselves and who do not at any time supply more electrical power than 5 megawatts of which not more than 2.5 megawatts is supplied to domestic consumers"; the 5 MW limit "is a measure of rate not volume" and is "absolute", and includes associated bodies corporate; "Class A does not allow any parties other than generators to operate as an exempt supplier"; a Class A supplier "cannot buy and resell power for back-up or top-up purposes" (the licensed supplier supplies the balance under a separate contract); "No, you do not currently have to register your reliance on the exemption with DESNZ, Ofgem or any other body"; exempt suppliers have ongoing duties under Schedule 2ZB (billing, contractual requirements, switching) and are subject to consumer protection, data protection and competition law; "We suggest that a supply contract is made"; using a broker "does not relieve generators from their legal responsibilities"; brokers must check they are not themselves "providing a supply of electricity under section 4(4)"; generation class exemption covers stations producing no more than 10 MW (or 50 MW where declared net capacity is under 100 MW) — [DESNZ licence exemptions FAQ (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/658d83aa01760d00135cf8d8/electricity-licence-exemptions-guidance.pdf)

- Centrica Hive ruling (4 March 2026, upheld): "Shrink your electricity bills by up to 94% in your first year with solar" — only 44% of customers achieved it, so not "a significant proportion"; the modelled basis and its assumptions were material information that had to be in the ad; breaches of rules 3.1, 3.3, 3.7, 3.9, 3.17, 3.22, 3.38 — [ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/centrica-hive-ltd-a25-1295069-centrica-hive-ltd.html)
- CAP news (9 April 2026): "up to"/"from" claims need evidence that "a significant proportion of customers can actually achieve the maximum saving"; if savings rely on modelled figures or specific datasets that context is "material information that needs to be made clear in the ad" — [ASA/CAP](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/bill-ieve-it-or-not-making-greener-homes-savings-claims-add-up.html)

- GGRS Energie ruling (26 Nov 2025): "Drastically Reduced Bills" unsubstantiated; factors affecting savings (geography, property, usage) were material information; rules 3.1, 3.3, 3.7, 3.9, 3.11, 3.17, 3.38 — [ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/ggrs-energie-ltd-a25-1295074-ggrs-energie-ltd.html)
- "Free": rule 3.23 — nothing can be described as free if the consumer pays anything beyond unavoidable response/collection costs; rule 3.24 — ads must make clear the commitment (purchase, subscription) needed; 3.25–3.26 on packages and trials; hidden costs or long-term contracts must be prominently disclosed — [ASA/CAP advice](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/use-of-free.html)
- CAP advice on domestic energy production (17 Apr 2025): savings claims "should not mislead by, for example, omitting to include purchase and installation costs" and must not overstate generation or offset gas costs — [ASA/CAP advice](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/environmental-claims-electricity-from-renewable-sources-domestic-energy-production.html)

### Inferences
- For the installer's website: the PPA itself needs no FCA wording, but any mention of leases/hire purchase/loans needs the introducer/credit-broker line and the regulated broker's name; and if sole-trader or small-partnership customers (farms, garages) might take finance of £25k or less, that agreement can be regulated consumer credit.
- The funder (not the installer) is the exempt supplier; a small installer should never describe itself as "supplying" electricity and should not sign PPAs in its own name unless it is prepared to be the generator/exempt supplier with Schedule 2ZB duties.
- Wording discipline for B2B pages: avoid "free solar panels" (rule 3.23, since the customer pays per kWh under a 25-year contract); prefer "no upfront cost"/"funded by our partner"; any "save up to X%" needs evidence that a significant proportion of comparable sites achieve X; state that savings are modelled, depend on self-consumption and tariff, and that the PPA rate escalates.

### Inferences (draft copy)
How a funded solar PPA works, in plain English:
"A Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) lets your business run on solar without buying the system. A specialist funder pays for the panels, inverters and installation, owns the equipment and looks after it for the length of the agreement — usually 25 years. We survey your site, design the system and install it. Under the PPA you buy the electricity the panels generate and you use on site, at a fixed price per kWh agreed before installation and set below what you currently pay the grid. The rate normally rises by a small fixed percentage (typically 2–3%) or in line with an inflation index each year, and the agreement says exactly how. Your existing electricity supplier keeps supplying whatever the panels do not cover. Monitoring, maintenance, repairs, inverter replacement and insurance are the funder's responsibility, so there is nothing to budget for. Because the funder owns the panels, they usually take the income from any surplus exported to the grid, and they will need a lease or licence over the roof area for the term. At the end of the agreement you can usually buy the system for a pre-agreed (often nominal) sum, extend the agreement, or ask for the equipment to be removed and the roof reinstated. If you move out or sell the building, the agreement can normally pass to the new occupier if they agree to it; otherwise the contract's early-termination terms apply, so read them before you sign. There is no upfront cost, but it is a long-term contract with a third party, and your savings depend on how much of the solar power you use on site."

Eligibility checklist (say "typical funder requirements — we confirm eligibility with our funding partners"):
- Roof or ground space for a system of about 100 kWp or more (roughly 600 m² of unshaded roof); some funders consider sites from about 70 kW.
- Annual electricity use of about 80,000–100,000 kWh or more, with most consumption during daylight hours, and a half-hourly meter.
- You own the freehold, or hold a lease with 15 or more years left and the landlord's consent to install.
- Trading for two or more years with filed accounts and a sound credit profile (funders run credit checks on the business that will buy the power).
- A structurally sound roof with 25 years of life left (funders may ask for a structural survey) and a grid connection that can accept the system (we handle the G99 application).
- Willingness to enter a long-term (typically 15–25 year) agreement and to grant the funder a roof lease or licence.
