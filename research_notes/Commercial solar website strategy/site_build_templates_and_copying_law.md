# Site build routes, solar templates, and the law on copying a competitor's website (UK focus, September 2026)

Scope note: this covers (a) what a new UK commercial solar + PPA installer may and may not imitate from another company's website, (b) solar-specific templates in 2026, (c) platform choice, (d) legitimate imagery/video sourcing, (e) UK costs and timelines, and (f) the compliance pages a UK limited company site must carry. All legal points are general information drawn from published sources; a solicitor should confirm anything contentious. Template links were live-checked on 28 September 2026 unless stated otherwise.

## Key Question 1: What can legally be imitated from another company's website, and what must not be copied (UK, with US notes)?

### Takeaway
UK copyright automatically protects the *expression* on a site (text, photographs, illustrations/icons, video, source code, original graphic design/GUI elements, databases), but not ideas, functionality, general page structure or navigation patterns; the "look and feel" of a website has never been squarely protected by a UK court, whereas copying photos or paragraphs of copy is routine infringement (a home-improvement firm paid £6,300 for 21 copied photos). Names, logos and a distinctive overall "get-up" are policed by trade mark law and passing off rather than copyright, and in the US the "look and feel" of a website can additionally be protected as trade dress.

### Cited Findings

**What UK copyright protects and how it arises**
- GOV.UK states copyright automatically protects "original literary, dramatic, musical and artistic work, including illustration and photography", "original non-literary written work, such as software, web content and databases", "film and television recordings" and "the layout of published editions of written, dramatic and musical works"; "You get copyright protection automatically - you do not have to apply or pay a fee." — [GOV.UK: Copyright](https://www.gov.uk/copyright)
- A UK law-firm guide lists as protected: "Written content and blog posts (literary works)", "Photographs, illustrations, logos and graphics (artistic works)", "Web page layouts and original design elements (can be protected where they amount to artistic works or a compilation)", "Videos and audio (films and sound recordings)", "Software code and scripts that you (or your developer) wrote (literary works)" and "Databases you created (potentially protected by copyright and the database right)". Not protected: "Copyright protects the expression of ideas, not the ideas themselves" and "Copyright does not protect names, slogans or the source-identifying function of your logo." Critically: "a competitor can build a site in a similar style or with a similar structure provided they do not copy your original content." It also warns "UK fair dealing is much narrower than the US concept of 'fair use'" and advises "If you're not sure, get permission or commission original work." — [Sprintlaw UK: Website copyright in the UK](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/website-copyright-in-the-uk-what-it-covers-and-how-to-protect-it/)
- Sprintlaw's companion guide on copied sites lists "website copy (text and articles)", "photographs and images", "graphics and illustrations", "video and audio content" and "code (in many cases), plus the way code is written" as the elements a copier infringes, and recommends trade mark registration for "your business name", "your logo" and "product or service name". — [Sprintlaw UK: Someone copied my entire website](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/someone-copied-my-entire-website/)
- Fosters Solicitors: "If you should pen a sparkling paragraph which enthusiastically captures the essence of your brand, only to see a competitor use the same wording on their website, leaflets or brochures, it is likely that the competitor in question will have infringed your copyright." Passing off exists "to prevent another business from unfairly attempting to align with it" where "consumers would be confused into thinking that goods or services offered by one company are the goods and services of another company." — [Fosters Solicitors](https://fosters-solicitors.co.uk/insight/plagiarism-and-unfair-association-by-competitors)
- Copyright "does not usually protect names or short phrases"; those are "more commonly dealt with through trade mark infringement or passing off." — [Sprintlaw UK: Copyright infringement in the UK](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/copyright-infringement-in-the-uk-what-businesses-should-know/)
- Database right (separate from copyright): "A property right ('database right') subsists ... in a database if there has been a substantial investment in obtaining, verifying or presenting the contents of the database", regardless of whether the database or its contents qualify for copyright. — [Copyright and Rights in Databases Regulations 1997, reg 13](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1997/3032/regulation/13)

**Look and feel, layout and structure (UK case law)**
- *Navitaire v easyJet* [2004] EWHC 1725 (Ch): copyright in software "did not protect against 'non-textual copying'"; what was copied "was merely the 'business logic' of the program ... which is not protected by copyright"; courts should be "astute not to extend copyright protection to purely functional effects, as opposed to actual code"; on screen displays, "Some of the displays were properly characterised as underlying ideas and so not protected, while others were artistic works in respect of which the action succeed[ed]." — [5RB case note](https://www.5rb.com/navitaire-inc-v-easyjet-airline-co-another/)
- Wikipedia's summary of the same case: character-based VT100 screens "did not attract copyright protection, but the GUI screens, involving design and artistic elements such as icons and layout, did qualify as artistic works and were infringed by the defendants copying several icons and screen layouts"; Navitaire "failed to obtain broad 'look and feel' protection". — [Wikipedia: Navitaire v easyJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navitaire_Inc_v_Easyjet_Airline_Co._and_BulletProof_Technologies,_Inc.)
- *THJ Systems v Sheridan* [2023] EWCA Civ 1354 (Court of Appeal): graphical user interfaces (risk/price charts) qualified as artistic works under the "author's own intellectual creation" test based on "Careful arrangement of component parts", "selection of fonts, colors, and command placement"; but "while the degree of visual creativity in the risk and price charts was low, there could still be copyright" with correspondingly narrow scope: only close copies infringe. — [Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/uk-court-of-appeal-rules-on-copyright-in-guis/)
- A Pillsbury practice note states "The issue of the look and feel of websites has yet to come before the courts in the UK", that "the English courts are less ready to attach copyright protection to the look and feel of software programs than the US", and cites *Easyjet Airline Co Ltd v Dainby* (a domain-name/passing off case) where "it was clear from the appearance of the website that the defendant had based the site on that operated by the plaintiff" and injunctive relief was granted. (Undated note; treat as background.) — [Pillsbury: The Look and Feel of Software](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/a/web/1353/43B42EE30058F9E47223237D4DA2EB30.pdf)
- Copyright Aid (UK volunteer copyright advice forum) on a cease-and-desist claiming a site's "layout, styling, and textual content" infringed: protecting overall "look and feel" through copyright is "difficult ground"; the "idea/expression dichotomy" applies; code is protected only when "a fairly substantial amount" is identical because "HTML and CSS have limited expression options"; it quotes *John Kaldor Fabricmaker UK Ltd v Lee Ann Fashions Ltd* [2014] EWHC 3779: "The stronger the prima facie case of copying which can be inferred from the similarities, the more compelling the defendant's evidence of independent design must be to rebut that inference." — [Copyright Aid forum](https://copyrightaid.co.uk/forum/viewtopic.php?t=2109)

**Passing off and get-up**
- The classic trinity from *Reckitt & Colman v Borden* ("Jif Lemon"): goodwill, misrepresentation, damage. Passing off can protect "trade dress (the whole external appearance or look-and-feel of a product, including any marks or other indicia used)" but "does not confer monopoly rights to any names, marks, get-up or other indicia"; remedies include injunctions, damages, account of profits. — [Wikipedia: Passing off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passing_off)
- LexisNexis: "A claim in passing off is based on the premise that no one has the right to represent their goods or services as being those of somebody else." — [LexisNexis: Introduction to passing off](https://www.lexisnexis.co.uk/legal/guidance/introduction-to-passing-off)

**A UK trades/home-services example of copied website content**
- *Absolute Lofts South West London Ltd v Artisan Home Improvements Ltd* [2015] EWHC 2608 (IPEC): a Bradford home-improvement firm "downloaded 21 of Absolute Lofts' photographs and had them posted on its own site"; after a threat it "replaced the photographs with images licensed from Shutterstock for £300"; the court awarded £300 notional licence-fee damages plus £6,000 additional damages for flagrancy (£6,300 total) because Artisan had made "unfair profits". — [Swan Turton](https://swanturton.com/additional-damages-of-20x-the-licence-fee-for-infringement-of-loft-conversion-photographs-absolute-lofts-v-artisan-home-improvements/); [8 New Square](https://8newsquare.co.uk/absolute-lofts-south-west-london-ltd-v-artisan-home-improvements-ltd-2015-ewhc-2608-ipec/); [NIPC Law](http://nipclaw.blogspot.com/2015/09/damages-for-infringing-copyright-in.html)

**US position (briefly)**
- *Ingrid & Isabel, LLC v Baby Be Mine, LLC* (N.D. Cal. 2014): the court held "the look and feel" of a website can constitute protectable trade dress; the alleged copying covered "the idea of putting its logo in pink-orange pastel feminine lettering", "the use of models with long wavy hair, shown from head to mid-thigh, wearing white tank tops and jeans" and the "general colors, patterns, fonts and wallpaper"; non-functional elements included "feminine script, the particular poses chosen for its models, and the color and pattern of the wallpaper"; secondary meaning was "strengthened by deliberate copying"; it was "one of the very first times that a court has protected the design of online stores." — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/advertising-marketing-branding/347268/look-and-feel-of-a-website-can-constitute-protectable-trade-dress); [Beliveau Law](https://www.beliveaulaw.net/2015/08/company-sued-for-copying-look-of-competitors-website/)
- *Blue Nile, Inc v Ice.com, Inc* (W.D. Wash. 2007) "addressed the issue of whether a party can bring trade dress and unfair competition claims along with a claim for copyright infringement when a competitor copies the 'look and feel' and the content of that party's website"; Blue Nile alleged copying of the "overall look and feel" of its diamond search pages. — [World Trademark Review](https://www.worldtrademarkreview.com/article/interaction-between-trade-dress-and-copyright-discussed-district-court); [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2575129/blue-nile-inc-v-ice-com-inc/)
- US trade dress elements: definition of the dress, non-functionality ("Functional elements cannot receive trade dress protection"), distinctiveness ("either being inherently distinctive or having obtained distinctiveness due to secondary meaning") and "likelihood of confusion by the consuming public"; "trade dress can also protect the look and feel of a website, if the website is not protected by copyright law." — [Mandour & Associates](https://www.mandourlaw.com/trade-dress-infringement/)
- US copyright: "Copyright exists from the moment the work is created", but "You will have to register, however, if you wish to bring a lawsuit for infringement of a U.S. work", and "Registered works may be eligible for statutory damages and attorney's fees". — [US Copyright Office FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-general.html)

**Risks beyond copyright when copying text/images**
- Google's spam policy on scraped content prohibits "Republishing content from other sites without adding any original content or value, or even citing the original source" and warns "Sites that violate our policies may rank lower in results or not appear in results at all." — [Google Search Essentials: Spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)
- Image-licensing enforcement agencies (Pixsy, Copytrack, PicRights) send demand letters to UK small businesses; a UK guide notes they "are real companies operating under real legal frameworks, but that does not mean every claim they send is automatically valid" and that in the UK such agencies "cannot actually initiate litigation for copyright infringement" themselves; reported demands sit in the hundreds to low thousands of pounds (one UK example: £550 for a low-traffic blog). — [Trust Your Website](https://trustyourwebsite.com/uk/en/guides/copytrack-picright-legit); [With Jack](https://withjack.co.uk/so-youve-received-an-unauthorised-use-of-image-letter/); [Copyright Aid: Pixsy demand](https://copyrightaid.co.uk/forum/viewtopic.php?t=3504)
- ASA (9 April 2026, greener-homes advice): savings claims must be "achievable"; "up to" claims need broad evidence; and imagery must match the claim — including "an image of an 8-panel system" while basing savings claims on a 10-panel system "is likely to be a problem"; material information such as modelled data should be disclosed. — [ASA: Bill-ieve it or not](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/bill-ieve-it-or-not-making-greener-homes-savings-claims-add-up.html)
- ASA rulings against solar advertisers: Centrica Hive's "Shrink your electricity bills by up to 94% in your first year with solar" was not adequately substantiated and breached the CAP Code rules on misleading advertising, substantiation, qualification and prices; GGRS Energie's "Drastically Reduced Bills" was likely to mislead. — [ASA ruling: Centrica Hive Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/centrica-hive-ltd-a25-1295069-centrica-hive-ltd.html); [ASA ruling: GGRS Energie Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/ggrs-energie-ltd-a25-1295074-ggrs-energie-ltd.html)
- Business-to-business marketing (the PPA/commercial audience) is covered by the Business Protection from Misleading Marketing Regulations 2008: advertising is misleading if it "deceives or is likely to deceive the traders to whom it is addressed ... and ... is likely to affect their economic behaviour or ... injures or is likely to injure a competitor"; it applies to "websites, emails, sales decks, social media and printed materials" and is enforced by Trading Standards and the CMA. — [Sprintlaw UK: BPMMR 2008](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/misleading-marketing-regulations-2008-business-protection/); [Business Companion: B2B marketing](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/fair-trading/business-to-business-marketing)
- The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 "prohibits fabricated endorsements and requires verification of testimonials." — [HWB Accountants: Legal requirements for company websites (2026)](https://www.hwb-accountants.com/news-guides/news/legal-requirements-for-company-websites-in-the-uk-2026-guide/)

### Inferences
- "Cloning the structure" lawfully means: (1) wireframe the target site's page architecture and section order (hero, sectors, process, case studies, financing, FAQ, CTA) as a *list of ideas*; (2) write every sentence from scratch; (3) source your own photography/stock/manufacturer assets; (4) build from a purchased template or your own design so no CSS/JS/icon files are lifted; (5) choose your own palette/typography combination and logo so the overall get-up is not confusable. Sprintlaw's "similar style or similar structure ... provided they do not copy your original content" and the Navitaire/THJ line (functionality and ideas unprotected; close copies of specific artistic screens protected) support this reading.
- Copying a colour scheme alone is low-risk under copyright but becomes risky if combined with the same fonts, layout, imagery style and naming so that a customer could think the two firms are connected (passing off) — the Ingrid & Isabel facts (matching colours, fonts, model styling) show what "too close" looks like in the US, and *easyJet v Dainby* shows a UK court will infer basing a site on a competitor's from appearance.
- Copying competitor copy is the single most likely way to get caught (text is easy to search), it invites Google demotion, and if the copied claims are savings/performance claims you inherit an ASA/BPMMR substantiation problem you cannot meet. Copying a competitor's installation photos is worse: it is copyright infringement *and* a misleading implication that the projects are yours.
- Because IPEC has a small-claims track and awards additional damages for flagrancy, a copied-photo dispute realistically costs a small firm several thousand pounds plus legal costs, not just a licence fee.

### Gaps
- No UK judgment squarely on "website look and feel" was found; the Pillsbury note stating the point "has yet to come before the courts" is undated, so a more recent decision could exist that was not surfaced.
- Detail of the *Blue Nile* holding (which claims survived on copyright pre-emption) was behind a paywall; *Salt Optics v Jand* and *Conference Archives v Sound Images* were not retrieved.
- No specific UK dispute over a copied *solar* installer website was found; the closest trades example is the loft-conversion photo case.

## Key Question 4: Where can the firm legitimately get commercial-solar imagery and video with no projects of its own, and how should founders be photographed?

### Takeaway
Use a layered plan: a half-day branded shoot of the founders (van, workwear, tools, training) for authenticity; manufacturer/partner-portal assets where the terms expressly allow marketing use (SolarEdge partner portal, K2 Systems press images with a copyright credit, SMA logo under its published licence); paid stock (Adobe Stock 10 assets £19.99/month) for hero rooftop/industrial shots; free stock (Unsplash/Pexels/Pixabay) only for secondary imagery with attention to model/brand releases; licensed drone clips (Pond5 from $15–$39, Artgrid $19.99/month) or own drone flying under CAA Operator/Flyer ID rules; and never present stock, renders or AI images as "our installations", because ASA rules apply regardless of how an image was made and a disclosure does not cure a misleading impression.

## Key Question 6: Which legal and compliance pages must a UK limited company's website carry (and MCS/RECC/HIES logo rules if certified)?

### Takeaway
A UK Ltd company must show its registered name, the part of the UK where registered, its registered number and registered office address on the website (Companies Act 2006 via the 2015 Trading Disclosures Regulations), plus the E-Commerce Regulations 2002 items (geographic address, email, VAT number if VAT-registered, clear prices) and the Provision of Services Regulations 2009 items (legal status, general terms, professional-indemnity insurer details). It also needs a UK GDPR privacy notice, a PECR-compliant cookie mechanism (ICO guidance finalised 29 April 2026: equal-prominence reject button; analytics/appearance cookies exempt if users get a simple way to object), accessible design under the Equality Act 2010, and, once certified, MCS/RECC logos used strictly per their brand rules (only while certified, only for certified services, unaltered, and for RECC linked to the RECC site).

### Cited Findings

**Company identity disclosures**
- Regulation 25 of the Company, LLP and Business (Names and Trading Disclosures) Regulations 2015: "Every company shall disclose the particulars set out in paragraph (2) on—(a) its business letters; (b) its order forms; and (c) its websites": "the part of the United Kingdom in which the company is registered", "the company's registered number", "the address of the company's registered office", plus status statements for exempt/CIC/investment companies; if share capital is mentioned "that disclosure must be as to paid up share capital". Regulation 28: failure without reasonable excuse is an offence by "the company; and every officer of the company who is in default", punishable by "a fine not exceeding level 3 on the standard scale" and "a daily default fine not exceeding one-tenth of level 3". — [SI 2015/17, Part 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/17/part/6)
- The registered *name* must also be displayed on websites under Part 6 of the same Regulations (ICAS reminder). — [ICAS: Business names and other disclosures](https://www.icas.com/news-insights-events/news/practice/business-names-and-other-disclosures-a-reminder)
- Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002, reg 6: a service provider must make available "easily, directly and permanently" its name, "geographic address" of establishment, email address, trade register details and registration number, any authorisation scheme, professional body details, and "the VAT number" where subject to VAT; where prices are quoted they must be "clearly and unambiguously" stated and say whether tax and delivery are included. — [SI 2002/2013, reg 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2013/regulation/6)
- Provision of Services Regulations 2009, reg 8: providers must make available their "name", "legal status and form", "geographic address", contact details, register details, competent authority details, VAT registration number, professional body/title, "General terms, conditions, and contractual provisions regarding jurisdiction", price where pre-determined, and "Professional liability insurance or guarantee details"; information counts as made available if provided on the provider's initiative, easily accessible at the place of service, or "electronic access". — [SI 2009/2999, reg 8](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2009/2999/regulation/8)
- HWB's 2026 checklist adds: contact email and phone, geographic address if selling online, VAT number where applicable, "All directors' names (if any listed)", a UK GDPR privacy policy, cookie consent for tracking pixels and profiling cookies, terms and conditions for service businesses, accessibility (alt text, assistive-technology compatibility), and fake-review prohibitions under the DMCC Act 2024; it cites a maximum data-protection penalty of "£17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover". — [HWB Accountants: Legal requirements for company websites in the UK (2026)](https://www.hwb-accountants.com/news-guides/news/legal-requirements-for-company-websites-in-the-uk-2026-guide/)
- Practical placement: the details "doesn't have to be on every page but it must be easily found"; the footer is common practice. — [PracticeWeb: Displaying company registration information](https://www.practiceweb.co.uk/knowledge/displaying-company-registration-information/)

**Privacy notice (UK GDPR Articles 13/14)**
- ICO: a privacy notice must "Say who you are and how individuals can contact you"; give DPO/representative contacts if applicable; "Explain why you use people's personal data. Be clear about each different purpose"; "Explain which lawful basis you are relying on"; legitimate interests where used; "Say who you share people's personal data with"; international transfers; "Say how long you will keep the personal data for"; "Tell people which rights they have"; how to withdraw consent; "Tell people that they can complain to a supervisory authority"; whether provision is statutory/contractual; and automated decision-making details. — [ICO: What privacy information should we provide?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/the-right-to-be-informed/what-privacy-information-should-we-provide/)

**Cookies / storage and access technologies (PECR as amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025)**
- ICO guidance on storage and access technologies, "finalized on 29 April 2026": consent is not needed under the communication, strictly necessary, "Statistical purposes" (with a "simple means of objecting"), "Appearance" (with a simple means of objecting) and emergency-assistance exceptions; consent mechanisms "must provide equal prominence for rejection and acceptance" and organisations "cannot pre-enable non-exempt technologies". — [ICO: Guidance on the use of storage and access technologies](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-the-use-of-storage-and-access-technologies/)
- Womble Bond Dickinson: the DUAA statistical exception covers storage "with a view to making improvements" and the appearance exception "to remember viewing preferences"; users need "a simple and free of charge means of objecting", which the ICO says can be toggles "defaulted to on but with an ability for users to switch them off at any time"; the rules "came into force on 5 February 2026"; DUAA aligned PECR fines with UK GDPR at "£17,500,000 or 4% of an undertaking's total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher" (previously £500,000). — [Womble Bond Dickinson: New exceptions for non-intrusive cookies](https://www.womblebonddickinson.com/uk/insights/articles-and-briefings/new-exceptions-non-intrusive-cookies-icos-updated-guidance-has)
- CookieYes' summary of the April 2026 guidance: "The reject option must be just as easy to find and use as the accept option"; "Pre-ticked boxes do not constitute consent"; under the statistical exception "The data collected must be aggregated and must not be used to identify individual users" and third-party analytics providers must act solely on your behalf; enforcement "tends to focus first on the largest and highest-traffic websites". (CookieYes states penalties are "currently" £500,000, which conflicts with the Womble/HWB £17.5m figure; the law-firm sources are more likely current.) — [CookieYes: UK ICO cookie guidance](https://www.cookieyes.com/blog/uk-cookie-guidance-ico-pecr/)
- Baseline ICO rule: consent requires "a clear positive action" such as ticking a box or clicking a link, and you must "tell people the cookies are there; explain what the cookies are doing and why". — [ICO: Cookies and similar technologies](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/cookies-and-similar-technologies/)
- The 2026 guidance treats cookies, pixels, fingerprinting, web storage and link decoration alike as "storage and access technologies". — [ConsentPixel: UK cookie law 2026](https://consentpixel.com/blogs/uk-cookie-law-pecr-2026/)

**Accessibility**
- The Equality Act 2010 applies to private businesses; service providers "must make reasonable adjustments to ensure disabled people can access their services", and "An inaccessible website can constitute a failure to make reasonable adjustments"; WCAG 2.2 AA is "the recognized benchmark" though the Act "does not explicitly specify conformance to WCAG"; EHRC can investigate and pursue court action. — [Level Access: UK digital accessibility requirements](https://www.levelaccess.com/blog/website-accessibility-laws-in-the-u-k/); [AudioEye: UK website accessibility law](https://www.audioeye.com/post/uk-website-accessibility-law/)

**B2B and consumer marketing rules**
- BPMMR 2008 governs advertising aimed at businesses (misleading and comparative advertising); enforced by Trading Standards and CMA. — [Sprintlaw UK: BPMMR 2008](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/misleading-marketing-regulations-2008-business-protection/)
- The CAP Code appendices explain the interplay with the DMCC Act and the BPRs. — [CAP Code Appendix 1: The DMCCA and the BPRs](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/appendix-1.html)

**MCS certification mark**
- MCS scope: "installations of small-scale renewables in both domestic and commercial settings up to a certain size – 50kW for electrical technologies, or 45kW for heating"; ongoing costs include "the £30 fee for every installation you register with MCS"; certification steps: preparation, application to a certification body, scheme checks, non-conformity resolution, signing the Installer Agreement. — [MCS: Becoming certified](https://mcscertified.com/installers/becoming-certified/)
- MCS Certified Brand Guidelines (Issue 5.0, November 2019): installers "should only use the MCS Certified Mark if you are currently certified as an Installer"; the Mark "should only appear on promotional materials (printed or digital) such as stationery, vehicles or advertising"; it "should only be used in connection with your certified installation services. It shouldn't be used to create the impression that other services have been certified by MCS if this is not the case"; reproduce only from approved EPS/PNG artwork obtained "from your Certification Body"; minimum width 10 mm; clear space equal to the height of the 'M'; never "redrawn, recoloured, stretched, squeezed or distorted", no effects, angles, opacity changes or graphic containers; use is governed by "the license agreement issued by your Certification Body on behalf of MCS". — [MCS Certified Brand Guidelines v5.0 (PDF)](https://www.szutest.cz/www/upload/categories/documents/20200210015603128.pdf)
- MCS: the brand pack is issued "directly from their certification body upon successful completion of the certification process and signing of the Installer Agreement"; "you can use the MCS Certification Mark in your marketing materials so long as you do so in accordance with MCS brand guidelines"; "MCS will take action against any misuse"; installer assets are password-protected. — [MCS: Use MCS to market your business](https://mcscertified.com/installers/becoming-certified/use-mcs-to-market-your-business-welcome-to-mcs/); [MCS assets for installers](https://mcscertified.com/installers/becoming-certified/use-mcs-to-market-your-business-welcome-to-mcs/mcs-assets-for-installers/)

**RECC / HIES consumer-code logos**
- RECC logo guidance (v2, 5 April 2013, still the linked document): the logo "may only be used by Code Members" and "may not be used by affiliates of Code Members, firms sub-contracting to or from Code Members or firms providing leads to or taking leads from Code Members, unless those firms are themselves Code Members"; "It is an offence under the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 to claim to be a signatory to a code of conduct when the trader is not or to display a trust mark, quality mark or equivalent without having obtained the necessary authorisation"; the image "should not be altered in any way"; the words must be legible (width not less than about 25 mm); "When used on a website the Logo must be linked to the Renewable Energy Consumer Code website"; allow white space of a sixth of the logo width; group it with MCS/Competent Person logos "and not with the logos of manufacturers"; do not imply endorsement "of a member's financial stability or the safety or suitability of specific goods"; misuse can lead to withdrawal of permission and disciplinary action; members must also use the TSI (CTSI) logo per TSI brand guidelines. — [RECC: Guidance on the use of the logo (PDF)](https://www.recc.org.uk/pdf/guidance-on-use-of-the-logo.pdf); [RECC member logos](https://www.recc.org.uk/member)
- HIES is a CTSI-approved consumer code "covering the installation of renewable energy and home energy efficiency products across the UK". — [Reonic: HIES vs RECC 2026](https://reonic.com/en-gb/blog/hies-vs-recc-consumer-code/)
- Podium's installer-site checklist: put "MCS certification front and centre" with RECC/HIES/TrustMark memberships visible. — [Podium Design](https://www.podiumdesign.co.uk/services/renewable-energy-website-design)

### Inferences
- Minimum footer/legal set for this company: (1) "[Company] Ltd is registered in England and Wales, company number X, registered office [address], VAT number Y" on every page footer; (2) Contact page with geographic address, email and phone; (3) Privacy notice covering enquiry forms, CRM (HubSpot/Pipedrive) processors, analytics and retention; (4) Cookie notice with equal-weight Accept/Reject and a toggle for analytics (or configure analytics to fit the statistical exception); (5) Terms of website use and a "Service information" page listing legal status, PI/public liability insurer and territorial cover (PSR 2009 reg 8), general terms and jurisdiction; (6) Accessibility statement (WCAG 2.2 AA target); (7) Complaints/ADR route once a consumer code applies.
- Because commercial arrays above 50 kW fall outside MCS installer scope, a commercial-only firm may never hold MCS; it must not display the MCS mark or use "MCS-accredited" wording until certified, and should not show manufacturer logos in a way that implies certification or partnership it does not hold (SMA's licence must be accepted first; RECC's guidance bans mixing accreditation logos with manufacturer logos).
- The "all directors' names" point in the HWB guide reflects the business-letter rule (if you name one director other than as signatory you must name all); on a website, an "About" page naming both founders is fine because both are named.

### Gaps
- HIES member logo usage rules were not retrievable (site blocks automated requests); confirm with HIES on joining.
- The Ofgem SEG page confirms eligibility up to 5 MW but the MCS-or-equivalent requirement for larger commercial systems was not captured; verify current SEG/export requirements for >50 kW installations.
- Whether the RECC logo guidance has been superseded since 2013 was not confirmed (the PDF is the version currently linked from RECC).
